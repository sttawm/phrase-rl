\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) are strikingly sensitive to
instruction phrasing and do not inherit the language robustness of the
vision-language models they are built on. A one-word edit can move
success by tens of points: $\pi_{0.5}$ turns on a LIBERO stove 100\% of
the time when told to ``switch on the stove'' and 2\% of the time when
told to ``switch on the hot plate'', and a $\pi_0$ checkpoint already
finetuned with rephrase augmentation still shows single-edit swings of up
to 61 points on SIMPLER's Bridge tasks. We characterize this sensitivity
with statistically tested single-edit swings and an oracle phrase search,
which shows that phrasing alone nearly closes the 21-point gap between
in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks. We then reduce it without
modifying the policy. Our key observation is that the sensitivity is
systematic enough to be written down: we score many phrasings of a few
training tasks, have a large language model distill the evidence into ten
to twenty natural-language rephrasing rules, and at deployment rewrite
each incoming instruction under these rules once, before it reaches the
frozen policy. The rules improve the frozen $\pi_0$ by 16 to 27\% relative
on twelve held-out tasks across adversarial, VLM-generated, and
human-generated phrasings, with gains concentrated on out-of-distribution
tasks and stable across three applier models and three independent
distillations. The method requires no retraining and no per-step
verification, and applies zero-shot to unseen tasks and instructions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Humans have long dreamed of general-purpose robots that help with everyday physical tasks; the dream remains elusive. Recent years have nonetheless brought enormous progress in generalist vision-language models (VLMs), and it is natural to build robot policies as extensions of them, in the form of vision-language-action models (VLAs). The hope is that a VLA inherits its VLM's capabilities, including robustness to how an instruction is phrased. It does not. Telling $\pi_{0.5}$ to ``switch on the stove'' succeeds 100\% of the time; ``switch on the hot plate'', 2\%. Telling $\pi_0$ that the ``purple eggplant goes on the sponge'' succeeds 69\% of the time on a SIMPLER task; dropping the color adjective, 8\%. No one who has used a modern language model would expect this.

A robot that executes a task only under particular wording is not a robot that non-experts can instruct. The standard remedy is training-time augmentation: relabeling demonstrations with additional phrasings so that the policy sees more surface forms of each instruction~\cite{kwok2026scaling, xiao2023dial}. It helps: the $\pi_0$ checkpoint we study was finetuned on Bridge with rephrase augmentation~\cite{intact}, which in our measurement is worth a 20\% relative improvement on naturally phrased requests over the otherwise-identical non-augmented checkpoint. Yet it still exhibits the swings above, and this residual sensitivity is the problem we address. Two deployment-side families exist, and neither fills the gap. Test-time verification~\cite{kwok2026scaling} selects among many phrase-action candidates at every inference step; it leaves the weights untouched but multiplies inference cost, and in our setting it does not improve over the unmodified policy. Instruction optimization~\cite{bhatia2026sarl, shodiev2026grounder, myers2024palo} searches for the phrasing that best steers a frozen policy on a given task, but needs online interaction with each task, so nothing it learns transfers to an unseen one. No existing method is simultaneously weight-free, applied once per episode, and zero-shot to unseen tasks.

% While we stop short of identifying the cause, we (1) demonstrate this sensitivity and (2) propose a simple, effective technique that reduces it without touching the policy's weights: we measure the robot's performance across many phrases per task, compile the results into an evidence file, and ask a frontier large language model (LLM) to distill ``rephrasing rules'' from the evidence. 
Our key observation is that a VLA's language sensitivity is systematic rather than idiosyncratic to each task, and can therefore be captured by a small set of explicit rules. We generate many phrasings of each of eight training tasks, roll each out in simulation, and record its success rate; a large language model then distills the scored phrasings into ten to twenty natural-language rephrasing rules, for example \textit{```coke' stays `coke', never `cola', `soda', or bare `can' ''}. At deployment, a rephraser conditioned on the rules and the scene image rewrites each incoming instruction once, before the episode begins, and passes the rewrite to the frozen VLA (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). The rules describe how the policy parses language in general, so they apply to tasks that contributed no evidence.

Before presenting the method, we characterize the sensitivity it targets, for $\pi_0$ on SIMPLER's Bridge tasks and $\pi_{0.5}$ on LIBERO. We report single-edit swings, sets of phrasings that differ by a minimal edit and whose success rates differ significantly under two-proportion tests over 72 ($\pi_0$) or 50 ($\pi_{0.5}$) rollouts per phrase, and an oracle phrase search that estimates the headroom phrasing alone leaves a frozen policy. Two findings shape the paper. First, oracle phrasing nearly closes the 21-point gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks seen under canonical instructions, so much of the apparent generalization gap is a phrasing gap. Second, on $\pi_{0.5}$ adversarial phrasing costs 50 points on tasks inside the finetuning set and nothing measurable outside it, suggesting the policy attends to language only where it has been trained to, consistent with prior reports that apparent robustness often reflects an ignored instruction~\cite{zhou2025liberopro, fei2025liberoplus}.

We evaluate on the rephrase-augmented $\pi_0$ across twelve sealed SIMPLER tasks that contributed no evidence, five in-distribution and seven out, under adversarial, VLM-generated natural, human-generated natural, and canonical phrasings. Rules distilled from eight tasks improve success by 16 to 27\% relative over the no-rephraser baseline on the adversarial and natural sets, with gains concentrated on out-of-distribution tasks. The effect is stable across three applier models (Claude, Gemini, and the open-weights Qwen3.5-9B) and three independent distillations, whose pooled success varies by at most 5 points, and out-of-finetune rulebooks consistently outperform in-finetune ones.

In summary, this paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item a statistically grounded characterization of language sensitivity in $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, comprising single-edit swings and oracle headroom, with the finding that phrasing accounts for much of the apparent in- versus out-of-distribution gap;
 \item a weight-free, once-per-episode method that distills rollout evidence into explicit rephrasing rules which transfer zero-shot to unseen tasks;
 \item an evaluation on held-out tasks across adversarial, VLM-generated, and human-generated phrasings, with three applier models and a comparison to test-time verification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Measuring language sensitivity. }
Embodied red teaming uses a VLM to search for natural-language
instructions that break language-conditioned robot policies, and finds
that state-of-the-art models fail or act unsafely on many of
them~\cite{karnik2024embodied}. LIBERO-PRO perturbs LIBERO along objects,
initial states, instructions, and environments; on the language axis it
finds that paraphrased instructions leave accuracy intact --- but so do
nonsensical ones, the policy executing the same memorized trajectory
regardless~\cite{zhou2025liberopro}. LIBERO-Plus charts the same
sensitivity across ten VLAs --- instruction perturbations cost between 14
and 50 points depending on the model --- and reaches the same verdict:
much of the apparent robustness traces to models ignoring the instruction
outright~\cite{fei2025liberoplus}. These studies report aggregate drops
over families of perturbations. Our single-edit swings are the
complementary, finer-grained observation: when the policy does attend to
language, a single word can move success by tens of points, and our oracle
search puts a number on how much of the apparent generalization gap is
phrasing.

\textbf{Training-time remedies. }
The standard fix is augmentation: relabeling demonstrations with
additional phrasings so that the policy sees many surface forms of each
instruction~\cite{kwok2026scaling}, or, as in DIAL, with VLM-generated
instructions that extend the training vocabulary~\cite{xiao2023dial}.
Steerable Policies goes further, training the VLA on synthetic commands at
several levels of abstraction --- subtasks, motions, grounded pixel
coordinates --- so that language can steer low-level
behavior~\cite{chen2026steerable}. All of these change the policy's
weights. The $\pi_0$ checkpoint we study was already trained with rephrase
augmentation~\cite{intact}; our method targets the sensitivity that
remains after such training, without training further.

\textbf{Deployment-time remedies. }
Two families leave the weights untouched. \emph{Test-time verification}
(CoVer) generates several rephrasings and several candidate actions at
every inference step and uses a contrastively trained verifier to select
the best phrase-action pair~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. It is our closest
comparison, and we evaluate against it in Section~\ref{sec:cover}; the
differences are that we act on phrasing only, never on actions, and that
we rephrase once per episode rather than at every step, so per-step
inference cost is unchanged. \emph{Instruction optimization} searches the
language input of a frozen policy: SARL learns by online reinforcement
which prompts steer a generalist policy to a given
task~\cite{bhatia2026sarl}, VLA Grounder trains an instruction-to-command
translator against rollout reward for a black-box
VLA~\cite{shodiev2026grounder}, and PALO optimizes language subtask
decompositions for few-shot adaptation~\cite{myers2024palo}. Each
optimizes per task through interaction with that task, so what it learns
does not transfer. Our rules are distilled once, offline, from eight tasks,
and apply zero-shot to unseen tasks.

\textbf{Language as the interface between VLMs and VLAs. }
RT-2 and PaLM-E showed that web-scale vision-language pretraining
transfers to robot control~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2, driess2023palme}, and
Hi Robot places a VLM above a VLA to turn open-ended prompts into
instructions the low-level policy can follow~\cite{shi2025hirobot}. SayCan
grounds an LLM planner in learned value functions so that proposed plans
respect the robot's skills~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, and Inner Monologue
closes the loop with execution feedback~\cite{huang2022inner}. These
systems decide \emph{which} instruction to issue; ours decides how to
\emph{word} it. The rephraser is the instruction-level analogue of
affordance grounding: rather than selecting which skill to invoke, it
learns which surface forms of an instruction this particular executor
parses reliably.

\section{Models and Benchmarks}

We work with two VLAs, $\pi_0$~\cite{pi0} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05}, each with a checkpoint inherited from prior work: $\pi_0$ finetuned on Bridge~\cite{intact} and $\pi_{0.5}$ finetuned on LIBERO~\cite{pi05}. All $\pi_0$ experiments use Bridge tasks and all $\pi_{0.5}$ experiments use LIBERO tasks. The $\pi_0$ checkpoint was finetuned with rephrase augmentation, i.e.\ on rephrasings of each task, precisely to reduce language sensitivity; it therefore already carries a mitigation, and we study what remains. In our own tests the augmentation is worth a 20\% relative improvement on natural rephrasings (28.4\% vs.\ 23.8\% over 192 phrases).

Success is measured in simulation. For SIMPLER's Bridge tasks we measure it at the 60th frame, the benchmark's default horizon; LIBERO's protocol is described below.

\subsection{Tasks and Canonical Instructions}

Each benchmark provides a set of simulatable tasks, and each task a canonical instruction.

\subsubsection{SIMPLER / Bridge}

SIMPLER~\cite{simpler} provides simulated replicas of real-robot evaluation setups; we use its Bridge~\cite{bridge} suite of WidowX tabletop tasks, such as ``put carrot on plate'' and ``put eggplant into yellow basket''. We use the four Bridge tasks shipped with the benchmark and construct 16 more from benchmark-provided primitives, for 20 simulatable tasks. Eight are \emph{in-vocabulary}: every noun in the canonical instruction appears in the Bridge training language. Success is determined procedurally by the benchmark. Our \emph{sealed} evaluation set fixes 12 of the 20 ex ante and holds them out of all evidence gathering; its five in-vocabulary and seven out-of-vocabulary tasks are the in-distribution and out-of-distribution strata reported in our figures.

\subsubsection{LIBERO}

LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero} consists of 130 simulatable tabletop tasks, such as ``put the bowl on the plate'' and ``put the moka pot on the stove'', grouped into four suites: libero\_spatial, libero\_object, and libero\_goal (10 tasks each), plus libero\_100, which splits into libero\_90 (90 tasks) and libero\_10 (10). We inherit a $\pi_{0.5}$ finetuned on every suite except libero\_90~\cite{pi05}, so libero\_90 tasks are \emph{out-of-finetune} and the rest \emph{in-finetune}. Success is again defined procedurally by the benchmark; each task runs to a suite-specific step limit and counts as successful once any step succeeds.



\section{Characterizing a VLA's Language Sensitivity}
\label{sec:minimal-pairs}

To give a concrete sense of the language sensitivity of $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, we present \emph{single-edit swings}: sets of phrases in which a minimal edit produces a large change in success rate. The sets are charted in Figs.~\ref{fig:pairs-bridge} and~\ref{fig:pairs-libero}, and the corresponding scenes are in Appendix~\ref{app:scenes}. We also estimate the headroom phrasing alone leaves a frozen VLA: how much would success improve if an \emph{oracle} chose the best phrase per task?

\subsection{Finding Single-Edit Swings}

Our search is simple. We first generate many rephrasings of each task (in fact, the same evidence files our rule distillers consume). With the help of an LLM we then propose candidate sets from the evidence, and we evaluate each phrase with many rollouts in simulation. We retain only sets in which the gap in success rate between the best and worst phrase is statistically significant, that is, unlikely to be explained by chance (each set's phrases and exact p-values are listed in the project repository\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/vla-rephrasing-artifacts}\label{fn:repo}}), and we manually remove sets whose gap could be explained by scene ambiguity. 
Significance is a two-proportion z test at $p<0.05$ (for $\pi_{0.5}$ we also require a gap of at least 18 points); no multiple-comparison correction is applied, so with 426 candidate pairs for $\pi_{0.5}$ about 21 significant swings are expected by chance alone. On $\pi_{0.5}$ this filter removed eight of the 23 significant sets: LIBERO scenes hold multiple objects, so a generalized noun (``bottle'', ``stand'') can match more than one of them, and a set is removed when the ambiguous phrasing is the one that loses --- its deficit may measure scene resolution rather than phrasing. Sets where the ambiguous phrasing wins are kept, and no $\pi_0$ set was removed, as Bridge scenes are single-target. 
For $\pi_0$ we evaluate each phrase 72 times (24 fixed scene layouts $\times$ 3 repetitions); for $\pi_{0.5}$, once on each of 50 layouts. Common random seeds per layout/repetition pair reduce measurement noise. Sets were harder to find for $\pi_{0.5}$ than for $\pi_0$.

\subsection{Patterns in the Swings}

Several patterns emerge. Some swings hold across tasks and even across
models: plate $\to$ dish costs $\pi_0$ 19 points and $\pi_{0.5}$ 56, and
``hot plate'' is catastrophic wherever it appears (2\% as a source noun,
0\% as a destination). Other swings do not transfer: adding ``purple'' to
the eggplant gains 61 points on one task and costs 30 on another, so no
static rule can satisfy both. Swings were also far easier to find on
in-finetune tasks: of the 426 candidate pairs our $\pi_{0.5}$ search
evaluated, 44\% of in-finetune pairs showed a significant swing
($|\Delta| \ge 18$ points, $p < 0.05$), versus 5\% of out-of-finetune
pairs --- a sign that finetuned performance rests on memorized phrasings,
which a single word can break. Color shows the clearest asymmetry between
the two models: on Bridge, an added color helps on out-of-vocabulary nouns
(black keyboard, $+42$) and hurts on in-vocabulary nouns (yellow
plate, $-22$); on LIBERO, an added color only ever hurts (black bowl,
$-20$). The common thread is plain naming: generic category words (can,
dish, drink, thing) lose to the specific everyday name --- even one the
training data never contained (pepsi beats soda by 39). Most
strikingly, a single letter matters: Pepsi $\to$ pepsi gains 44 points,
suggesting token-level sensitivity.

The search is simple: for a task, a VLM generates 16 rephrasings, each is scored by several rollouts, and the VLM then generates 16 new rephrasings conditioned on all scored phrases so far; after three rounds we select the best-scoring phrase.

\section{Reducing Language Sensitivity with LLM-Distilled Rephrasing Rules}

We now present our method for reducing this sensitivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). At a high level it is simple: generate many rephrasings of many tasks, score each phrase, present the scored phrases to an LLM, and receive a set of rules that specify how to rephrase an incoming instruction. Unlike per-task optimization, the rules must hold across all tasks at once.

\subsection{Gathering Evidence}

Rules are derived from the \emph{evidence tasks}: the eight simulatable Bridge tasks outside the sealed set, plus non-simulatable tasks with real-world expert trajectories. For each evidence task, we prompt a VLM to generate 16 \emph{natural} rephrasings, worded the way a person might request the task, and 8 \emph{adversarial} rephrasings, convoluted and overly complicated. For ``put carrot on plate'', a natural rephrasing is ``Could you move the carrot to the plate?'' and an adversarial one is ``Take the long orange item typically eaten by rabbits and ensure it ends up supported by the circular dishware.'' We also include every phrase measured during oracle search, mostly benign. The generation prompt is in the repository.\footref{fn:repo}

For non-simulatable tasks we use a proxy: the gripper error between the VLA's predicted action and the ground-truth action, averaged over 4 frames in each of 16 expert trajectories (64 measurements). On simulatable tasks this proxy correlates reasonably well with rollout success.

\subsection{Distilling Rules}

We prompt Claude to derive rules from the evidence with the prompt provided in the project repository.\footref{fn:repo} We test three rulebook types, distinguished by the evidence they see: \emph{in-finetune}, \emph{out-of-finetune}, and \emph{both}. Because distillation is stochastic, we distill each type three times and average their results in evaluation. Rulebooks contain 10 to 20 rules.

For rulebooks that see in-finetune phrases, we also provide the full set of finetuning instructions so that the distiller can use corpus statistics such as how often each object occurs and the most common phrase structures. The first rulebook of each type was distilled with Claude Fable and the remaining two with Claude Opus 5; our results do not suggest this choice mattered.

\subsection{Applying Rules}

Rules are applied once per episode, before the robot moves, by one of three \emph{appliers}: Claude, Gemini, or the open-weights Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen}. Besides the instruction and the rules, the applier receives a reasoning trace produced by Gemini 3.5 Flash (temperature 0.4) from the incoming phrase and the scene image; the applier itself never sees the image. The appliers are Claude Fable 5 (effort high; max on the human-generated set), Gemini through the gemini-pro-latest alias with a 16{,}384-token thinking budget, and Qwen3.5-9B with greedy decoding. \label{sec:rules-results}

We evaluate rulebooks on the rephrase-augmented $\pi_0$ over the 12 sealed Bridge tasks and 24 fixed layouts. The layouts are SIMPLER's deterministic object-initialization episodes 0--23, shared by every arm. \todo{state when the sealed split was fixed relative to evidence gathering}

\subsection{Phrase Sets}
\label{sec:phrase-sets}

We evaluate on four phrase sets: adversarials, VLM-generated naturals, human-generated naturals, and canonicals. Adversarial and VLM-generated natural phrases are produced by Gemini 3.5 Flash from the scene image, with the prompt in the project repository; adversarials keep the goal but are awkwardly worded, and VLM-generated naturals mimic how a person might request the task. Human-generated naturals were collected by survey from friends and family, who watched a successful video demonstration of each task and were asked to request it of an 8-year-old, an adult, and a robot.

Per task we evaluate 6 adversarial phrases (72 in total), roughly 16 VLM-generated naturals (186), and roughly 30 human-generated naturals (363). We also evaluate each task's canonical instruction, but with one phrase per task the canonical results (Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}) are statistically underpowered.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against three baselines. The \emph{no-rephraser} baseline passes the incoming instruction to the VLA unchanged. The \emph{no-rules rephraser} (the scaffold in Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}) runs the same applier with a single generic rule (see the project repository), isolating the effect of the distilled rules from that of rephrasing itself. CoVer~\cite{kwok2026scaling} uses a contrastively trained verifier to select, at every step, the highest-scoring phrase/action pair from 40 candidates (8 rephrasings $\times$ 5 actions). The paired construction and permutation test behind every reported p-value are described in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\subsection{Results}

The out-of-finetune rulebook is consistently the best type; the one exception is the canonical set, where the in-finetune rulebook is best, but that set is underpowered. Gains concentrate on out-of-distribution tasks. With out-of-finetune rulebooks, the Claude applier improves on the no-rephraser baseline by 20 to 26\% relative across the adversarial and natural sets (Table~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline}), and on the no-rules rephraser by 26\% on adversarials, 11\% on human-generated naturals, and 4.7\% on VLM-generated naturals (Table~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold}); Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard} gives the full results. Across the three independent draws of a rulebook type, pooled success varies by 0.7 to 5.1 points (median range 3.0), small relative to these effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Much remains. Rulebooks distilled from more tasks may yield larger
gains, and a more formal reinforcement-learning treatment of phrasing is
appealing, though it would likely demand far more simulatable tasks than
we worked with. Our in-finetune rulebooks underperformed, possibly
because of a weak proxy score; a cheaper, better proxy would avoid the
cost of full rollouts. We evaluated the method on a single VLA and only
in simulation; other VLAs and real robots are natural next steps. Most
importantly, language sensitivity should be measured systematically
across models. Explaining it --- attributing the sensitivity to a
cause --- would be the most exciting outcome of all.

\section{Statistical Tests}
\label{app:stats}

For a comparison between two arms, the paired unit is the \emph{base
instruction}: $d_i = c_i - b_i$, the two arms' success rates on base $i$
over the same fixed grid of scene layouts (rulebook arms average their three draws
before pairing). Common random numbers make the two arms face identical
scenes, so scene difficulty cancels within each $d_i$. Success is \emph{base-weighted} throughout (Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}): rollouts are averaged per task and phrase and then over base instructions, so each base counts equally however many bases an applier collapses onto the same rewrite. The reported
p-value is a two-sided sign-flip permutation test on the mean of the
$d_i$ (20{,}000 resamples; exact enumeration when $n \le 12$). The number
of bases is the sample size ($n = 186$ VLM-generated natural, $363$
human-generated natural, $72$ adversarial, $12$ canonical); layouts and
repetitions sharpen each $d_i$ but do not add to $n$.
Tables~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline} and~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold} report
each effect as a relative change: the mean paired difference as a percent
of the baseline's pooled rate (for the no-rephraser: adversarial
24.5\%, human-generated 23.0\%, VLM-generated 26.0\%).
