\section{Introduction}

Humans have long dreamed of general-purpose robots that help with everyday physical tasks. Recent years have brought enormous progress in generalist vision-language models (VLMs). It is no surprise, then, that we aim to build generalist physical robots --- vision-language-action models (VLAs) --- as extensions of modern VLMs, hoping to leverage and extend their capabilities. But do VLAs fully utilize the capabilities of their underlying VLMs? We notice that language-conditioned robots (or, at least, those involved in our experiments) can still be very sensitive to variations in phrasing. And this is contrary to what one might expect after using a modern language model.

While we stop short of identifying the cause, we (1) aim to demonstrate this language sensitivity, and we (2) propose a simple, effective technique that reduces a robot's language sensitivity after observing its performance on a small set of tasks. Concretely, we measure the robot's performance across many phrasings of a small set of tasks, compiling the result into an evidence file, and then ask a frontier LLM to generate a set of ``rephrasing rules'' based on the evidence. Then, when the VLA is given a language instruction, we pass the instruction, scene image, and rules to a rephraser, whose output goes to the VLA.

We evaluate in simulation on a variety of phrasing categories --- awkward, adversarially phrased instructions, naturally-phrased instructions generated by a VLM, and naturally-phrased instructions written by humans --- and see 16--27\% relative improvements for our best rulebook type over the un-rephrased $\pi_0$ baseline on SIMPLER's Bridge tasks.




\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Sensitivity. }
Embodied red teaming searches for natural-language instructions that break
robot policies~\cite{karnik2024embodied}. LIBERO-PRO perturbs LIBERO along
objects, initial states, instructions, and environments; on the language
axis it finds that paraphrased instructions leave accuracy intact --- but
so do nonsensical ones, the policy executing the same memorized trajectory
regardless~\cite{zhou2025liberopro}. LIBERO-Plus charts this
sensitivity across ten VLAs --- instruction perturbations cost between 14
and 50 points depending on the model --- and reaches the same verdict:
much of the apparent robustness traces to models ignoring the instruction
outright~\cite{fei2025liberoplus}. Our single-edit swings are the complementary
observation: when the policy does attend to language, one word can move
success by tens of points.

\textbf{Reducing Language Sensitivity. }
Recent works improve VLA performance by augmenting training data with
additional rephrases of training instructions~\cite{kwok2026scaling};
DIAL relabels demonstrations with VLM-generated instructions to the same
end~\cite{xiao2023dial}. Steerable Policies trains the VLA on synthetic
commands at multiple levels of abstraction --- subtasks, motions, grounded
pixel coordinates --- so that language can steer low-level
behavior~\cite{chen2026steerable}.

\textbf{Test-time verification. }
In conjunction with the above, a frozen VLA's performance can be improved by generating many phrase-action pairs at runtime and then using a pre-trained model to select the best candidate ~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. This method is our closest known comparison, yet we operate solely on task-phrasing; that is, we do not explicitly select good candidate actions. Moreover, though generating multiple phrase-action pairs at each inference step is relatively inexpensive, we avoid this cost by generating our rephrase just once, at the beginning of the episode.

\textbf{Optimizing instructions for a frozen policy. }
SARL learns by online reinforcement which prompts steer a frozen generalist
policy to a given task~\cite{bhatia2026sarl}; VLA Grounder trains an
instruction-to-command translator against rollout reward for a black-box
VLA~\cite{shodiev2026grounder}; PALO optimizes language subtask
decompositions for few-shot adaptation~\cite{myers2024palo}. Each optimizes
per task through interaction; our rules are distilled once, offline, and
apply zero-shot to unseen instructions.

\textbf{Using a VLM to improve VLA performance. }
Another body of work incorporates a VLM in some capacity to improve the performance of a downstream VLA \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme,shi2025hirobot}.

\textbf{Accounting for robot capabilities. }
SayCan grounds an LLM planner in learned value functions so that proposed
plans respect the robot's skills~\cite{ahn2022saycan}; Inner Monologue
closes the loop with execution feedback~\cite{huang2022inner}. Our
rephraser is the instruction-level analogue: rather than selecting
\emph{which} skill to invoke, it learns which \emph{surface forms} of an
instruction this executor parses reliably.

\section{Setup}

Throughout, we work with two modern VLAs: $\pi_0$~\cite{pi0} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05}. All of our $\pi_0$ experiments are conducted on the Bridge dataset, and all of our $\pi_{0.5}$ experiments are conducted on the LIBERO dataset. Both checkpoints are inherited from prior work: $\pi_0$ finetuned on Bridge~\cite{intact}, $\pi_{0.5}$ on LIBERO~\cite{pi05}. Moreover, the finetuned version of $\pi_0$ that we use is explicitly trained on rephrases of each task in order to reduce its language sensitivity. That is, $\pi_0$ already carries a mitigation for language sensitivity; we study what remains. In our own tests, the rephrase augmentation is worth a 20\% relative improvement on natural rephrasings (28.4\% vs 23.8\% pooled success on a 192-phrase natural-rephrase set, against the otherwise-identical non-augmented checkpoint); we seek to further improve robustness with rules.

In our experiments, we measure the success of these VLAs in simulation. One design choice: at which frame is success measured? For SIMPLER's Bridge tasks, we measure success at the 60th frame of a trajectory, the benchmark's default horizon.

\subsection{Tasks}

Each benchmark provides a set of simulatable tasks, and each task a canonical instruction.

\subsubsection{Bridge / SIMPLER}

SIMPLER~\cite{simpler} provides simulated replicas of real-robot evaluation setups; we use its Bridge~\cite{bridge} suite of WidowX tabletop tasks, such as ``put carrot on plate'' and ``put eggplant into yellow basket''. We use the four Bridge tasks shipped with the benchmark and construct 16 more from benchmark-provided primitives, for 20 simulatable tasks in total. Of these, eight are in-vocabulary: every noun in the canonical instruction appears in the Bridge training language. Task success is procedurally determined by the benchmark. Our sealed evaluation set fixes 12 of the 20 ex ante --- five in-vocabulary, seven out --- and these strata are the in-distribution / out-of-distribution splits reported in our figures.

\subsubsection{LIBERO}

LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero} consists of 130 simulatable tabletop tasks. Examples include ``put the bowl on the plate'' and ``put the moka pot on the stove''. Its tasks are grouped into four suites --- libero\_spatial, libero\_object, and libero\_goal (10 tasks each), plus libero\_100, which splits into libero\_90 (90 tasks) and libero\_10 (10). We inherit a $\pi_{0.5}$ finetuned on every suite except libero\_90~\cite{pi05}. As in SIMPLER, task success is defined procedurally by the benchmark's code. Each LIBERO task runs to a suite-specific step limit and counts as successful once any step succeeds.



\section{Characterizing a VLA's Language Sensitivity}
\label{sec:minimal-pairs}

To give a concrete sense of the language sensitivity of $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, we present \emph{single-edit swings}: sets of phrases in which a minimal edit produces a large performance swing. The sets are listed in Fig.~\ref{fig:pairs-bridge} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pairs-libero}, and the corresponding scenes are in the appendix. 
Furthermore, we estimate the headroom that phrasing alone leaves a frozen VLA: if an \textit{oracle} chose the best phrase per task, how much would its success rate improve?

\subsection{Finding Single-Edit Swings}

Our search algorithm is simple: first we generate many rephrases for each task (in fact, we use the exact evidence files used by our rules' distillers). Then, with the help of an LLM, we propose sets to test, based on the evidence, and we evaluate each phrase with many attempts in simulation. After gathering success rates per phrase, we retain only those sets where the difference in accuracy between the best and worst phrase is statistically significant, that is, they are unlikely to be explained by chance (see the lists for their exact p-scores). We manually filter out phrase sets with large gaps that are potentially explained by scene-based ambiguity. For $\pi_0$, we evaluate each phrase 72 times (24 layouts by 3 repetitions). For $\pi_{0.5}$, we evaluate each phrase once on each of 50 layouts. To further reduce noise in our measurements, we use common random seeds per layout / repetition pair. We note that it was qualitatively more challenging to find these phrase sets for $\pi_{0.5}$ than for $\pi_0$.

\subsection{Oracle Phrase Search}

To further quantify language sensitivity, for each task, we compare its ``best-performing'' phrase to its canonical phrase (see Setup for the definition of canonical phrase). To find the ``best'' phrase for a task, we employ a simple algorithm. For a fixed task, we first generate 16 rephrases using a VLM. We then score each phrase by rolling it out multiple times. Then, we ask the VLM to generate 16 new phrases based on all previously scored phrases. We repeat this process three times, and select the best-performing phrase. See Fig.~\ref{fig:pi0-conditions} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pi05-fourtier} for a comparison of the success rates of oracle phrases to canonicals, adversarials, and naturals. For both models, oracle phrases reach significantly higher success rates than the other phrase sets. By this measure, $\pi_0$ is more sensitive to phrasing than $\pi_{0.5}$, consistent with our experience searching for single-edit swings.

\section{Reducing Language Sensitivity with LLM-Distilled Rephrasing Rules}

We now present our method for reducing this sensitivity. An overview of our method is given in Fig.~\ref{fig:method}. At a high level, it is quite simple: generate many rephrases across many tasks, score each phrase, present these scored phrases to an LLM, and then receive a set of rules specifying how to rephrase an incoming language instruction. Unlike per-task optimization, the rules must hold across all tasks at once.

\subsection{Gathering Evidence: Generating and Scoring Phrases}

In order to derive phrasing rules, we first gather a large set of phrases. For each task in our training set, we prompt a VLM to generate 16 ``natural'' phrases --- e.g. the way a human might naturally request a task --- and 8 adversarial phrases --- e.g. a convoluted, overly complicated task request. For example, a natural rephrase of ``put carrot on plate'' is ``Could you move the carrot to the plate?'', whereas an adversarial one is ``Take the long orange item typically eaten by rabbits and ensure it ends up supported by the circular dishware.'' We also include all phrases measured during oracle search (these tend to be more benign rephrases). See the appendix for the prompt used to generate natural and adversarial phrases.

\subsubsection{Scoring Phrases}

Rule-distillation requires that each phrase gets a score, so that phrases are comparable. For simulatable tasks, we simply simulate each task / phrase pair multiple times to get a success rate. However, we also aim to measure phrases for non-simulatable tasks: these tasks are equipped with real-world expert trajectories. For these, we score rephrases using a simple metric that acts as a proxy for success rate. For each phrase, we calculate the gripper error between the ground truth action and the VLA's predicted action over 16 trajectories at 4 frames per trajectory. This gives us a set of 64 measurements which we average and use as the score. When using this proxy to measure task / phrase pairs that are simulatable, we see that their scores correlate relatively well with actual rollout success.

\subsection{Distilling Rules}

To distill the evidence into a set of rules, we prompt Claude to derive rules with the prompt provided in the project repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/sttawm/phrase-rl/tree/main/paper/prompts}} We test three rulebook types, distinguished by the evidence they see: in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both. Since rule-distillation is stochastic, we distill each type of rulebook three times, and during evaluation we average their results. In our experiments, rulebooks contain somewhere between 10 and 20 rules.

For rulebooks distilled from phrases from the finetuning dataset, we further provide the full set of finetuning phrases so that the distiller may make use of corpus statistics such as how frequently objects occur in the finetuning data, the most common phrase structures, etc. Due to cost constraints, we use Claude Fable to generate the first three rulebooks (one each of in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both), and then we repeat twice more with Claude Opus 5. Our results do not suggest this choice mattered.

All distilled rulebooks are available in the project
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/sttawm/phrase-rl/tree/main/paper/rules}}

\subsubsection{Applying Rules}

Rephrasing rules are applied just once before the robot's trajectory begins. Alongside the instruction and rules, the applier receives a reasoning trace generated from the incoming phrase and a scene image. These traces are generated by gemini-3.5-flash. The rules are applied greedily, and the output phrase is given directly to the VLA, after which the robot performs the task as usual.



\section{Evaluating the Rulebooks}
\label{sec:rules-results}

We evaluate $\pi_0$, finetuned on Bridge with rephrase augmentation, in simulation with SIMPLER tasks.

\subsection{Phrase Sets}

We evaluate on four different phrase sets: adversarials, VLM-generated naturals, human-generated naturals, and canonicals. Adversarial and VLM-Generated Natural phrases are generated by gemini-3.5-flash, given the scene image, using the prompt given in the project repository. Adversarial phrases retain the goal, but are awkwardly worded. VLM-generated naturals mimic the way a human might request a task. Human-generated naturals are gathered from friends and family via survey: the user is presented with a successful video demonstration of each task and asked to request the task of an 8-year-old, an adult, and a robot. We evaluate 6 adversarial phrases per task (72 in total), roughly 16 phrases per task for the VLM-generated naturals (186 in total), and roughly 30 phrases per task for the human-generated naturals (363 in total). We additionally evaluate the SIMPLER environment's canonical instruction accompanying each task, but we note that at just one phrase per task, one should be cautious in assigning significance to the canonical category's results (the canonical panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}).

\subsection{Baselines}

We evaluate against three baselines: no rephraser, no-rules rephraser, and CoVer. The no-rephraser does not rephrase at all and passes the incoming instruction directly to the VLA. The no-rules rephraser contains a single simple rule (see the project repository). CoVer uses a contrastively trained verifier to continuously select its verifier's highest-scoring phrase / action pair from a set of 40 (8 rephrases by 5 actions each). Each task is evaluated on 24 different layouts of the scene. The paired construction and permutation test behind
every reported p-value are described in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\subsection{Results}

Our best-performing rulebook type is consistently the out-of-finetune rulebook (the exception is the canonical set, where the in-finetune rulebook performs best; however, we note that at just a single phrase per task, our evaluation of canonical phrases is statistically underpowered). The gains concentrate on out-of-distribution tasks. With the out-of-finetune rulebooks, the Claude applier increases performance, on average, by more than 20\% across adversarial and natural phrases when compared to the no-rephraser baseline. When compared to the no-rules rephraser baseline, it improves performance by 26\% on adversarials, 11\% on human-generated naturals, and 4.7\% on VLM-generated naturals. See Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard} and Tables~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline} and~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold} for the detailed set of results.

\section{Statistical Tests}
\label{app:stats}

For a comparison between two arms, the paired unit is the \emph{base
instruction}: $d_i = c_i - b_i$, the two arms' success rates on base $i$
over the same pinned layout grid (rulebook arms average their three draws
before pairing). Common random numbers make the two arms face identical
scenes, so scene difficulty cancels within each $d_i$. The reported
p-value is a two-sided sign-flip permutation test on the mean of the
$d_i$ (20{,}000 resamples; exact enumeration when $n \le 12$). The number
of bases is the sample size ($n = 186$ VLM-generated natural, $363$
human-generated natural, $72$ adversarial, $12$ canonical); layouts and
repetitions sharpen each $d_i$ but do not add to $n$.
Tables~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline} and~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold} report
each effect as a relative change: the mean paired difference as a percent
of the baseline's pooled rate (adversarial 24.5\%, human-generated
naturals 23.0\%, LLM-generated naturals 26.0\%; scaffold rates per
applier and condition).
