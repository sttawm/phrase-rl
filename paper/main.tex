\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) are strikingly sensitive to
instruction phrasing and do not inherit the language robustness of the
vision-language models they are built on. A one-word edit can move
success by tens of points: $\pi_{0.5}$ turns on a LIBERO stove 100\% of
the time when told to ``switch on the stove'' and 2\% of the time when
told to ``switch on the hot plate'', and a $\pi_0$ checkpoint already
finetuned with rephrase augmentation still shows single-edit swings of up
to 61 points on SIMPLER's Bridge tasks. We characterize this sensitivity
with statistically tested single-edit swings and an oracle phrase search,
which shows that phrasing alone nearly closes the 21-point gap between
in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks. We then reduce it without
modifying the policy. Our key observation is that the sensitivity is
systematic enough to be written down: we score many phrasings of a few
training tasks, have a large language model distill the evidence into ten
to twenty natural-language rephrasing rules, and at deployment rewrite
each incoming instruction under these rules once, before it reaches the
frozen policy. The rules improve the frozen $\pi_0$ by 16 to 27\% relative
on twelve held-out tasks across adversarial, VLM-generated, and
human-written phrasings, with gains concentrated on out-of-distribution
tasks and stable across three applier models and three independent
distillations. The method requires no retraining and no per-step
verification, and applies zero-shot to unseen tasks and instructions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Humans have long dreamed of general-purpose robots that help with everyday physical tasks; the dream remains elusive. Recent years have nonetheless brought enormous progress in generalist vision-language models (VLMs), and it is natural to build robot policies as extensions of them, in the form of vision-language-action models (VLAs). The hope is that a VLA inherits its VLM's capabilities, including robustness to how an instruction is phrased. It does not. Telling $\pi_{0.5}$ to ``switch on the stove'' succeeds 100\% of the time; ``switch on the hot plate'', 2\%. Telling $\pi_0$ that the ``purple eggplant goes on the sponge'' succeeds 69\% of the time on a SIMPLER task; dropping the color adjective, 8\%. No one who has used a modern language model would expect this.

A robot that executes a task only under particular wording is not a robot that non-experts can instruct. The standard remedy is training-time augmentation: relabeling demonstrations with additional phrasings so that the policy sees more surface forms of each instruction~\cite{kwok2026scaling, xiao2023dial}. It helps: the $\pi_0$ checkpoint we study was finetuned on Bridge with rephrase augmentation~\cite{intact}, which in our measurement is worth a 20\% relative improvement on naturally phrased requests over the otherwise-identical non-augmented checkpoint. Yet it still exhibits the swings above, and this residual sensitivity is the problem we address. Two deployment-side families exist, and neither fills the gap. Test-time verification~\cite{kwok2026scaling} selects among many phrase-action candidates at every inference step; it leaves the weights untouched but multiplies inference cost, and in our setting it does not improve over the unmodified policy. Instruction optimization~\cite{bhatia2026sarl, shodiev2026grounder, myers2024palo} searches for the phrasing that best steers a frozen policy on a given task, but needs online interaction with each task, so nothing it learns transfers to an unseen one. No existing method is simultaneously weight-free, applied once per episode, and zero-shot to unseen tasks.

% While we stop short of identifying the cause, we (1) demonstrate this sensitivity and (2) propose a simple, effective technique that reduces it without touching the policy's weights: we measure the robot's performance across many phrases per task, compile the results into an evidence file, and ask a frontier large language model (LLM) to distill ``rephrasing rules'' from the evidence. 
Our key observation is that a VLA's language sensitivity is systematic rather than idiosyncratic to each task, and can therefore be captured by a small set of explicit rules. We generate many phrasings of each of eight training tasks, roll each out in simulation, and record its success rate; a large language model then distills the scored phrasings into ten to twenty natural-language rephrasing rules, for example \textit{```coke' stays `coke', never `cola', `soda', or bare `can' ''}. At deployment, a rephraser conditioned on the rules and the scene image rewrites each incoming instruction once, before the episode begins, and passes the rewrite to the frozen VLA (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). Because the rules describe how the policy parses language in general rather than what works for one task, they apply to instructions and tasks that contributed no evidence.

Before presenting the method, we characterize the sensitivity it targets, for $\pi_0$ on SIMPLER's Bridge tasks and $\pi_{0.5}$ on LIBERO. We report single-edit swings, sets of phrasings that differ by a minimal edit and whose success rates differ significantly under two-proportion tests over 72 ($\pi_0$) or 50 ($\pi_{0.5}$) rollouts per phrase, and an oracle phrase search that estimates the headroom phrasing alone leaves a frozen policy. Two findings shape the paper. First, oracle phrasing nearly closes the 21-point gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks seen under canonical instructions, so much of the apparent generalization gap is a phrasing gap. Second, on $\pi_{0.5}$ adversarial phrasing costs 50 points on tasks inside the finetuning set and nothing measurable outside it, suggesting the policy attends to language only where it has been trained to, consistent with prior reports that apparent robustness often reflects an ignored instruction~\cite{zhou2025liberopro, fei2025liberoplus}.

We evaluate on the rephrase-augmented $\pi_0$ across twelve sealed SIMPLER tasks that contributed no evidence, five in-distribution and seven out, under adversarial, VLM-generated natural, human-written natural, and canonical phrasings. Rules distilled from eight tasks improve success by 16 to 27\% relative over the un-rephrased baseline on the adversarial and natural sets, with gains concentrated on out-of-distribution tasks. The effect is stable across three applier models (Claude, Gemini, and the open-weights Qwen3.5-9B) and three independent distillations, whose pooled success varies by at most 5 points, and rulebooks distilled from out-of-finetune evidence consistently outperform those distilled from in-finetune evidence.

In summary, this paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a statistically grounded characterization of language sensitivity in $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, comprising single-edit swings and oracle headroom, with the finding that phrasing accounts for much of the apparent in- versus out-of-distribution gap;
    \item a weight-free, once-per-episode method that distills rollout evidence into explicit rephrasing rules which transfer zero-shot to unseen tasks;
    \item an evaluation on held-out tasks across adversarial, machine-generated, and human-written phrasings, with three applier models and a comparison to per-step test-time verification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Measuring language sensitivity. }
Embodied red teaming uses a VLM to search for natural-language
instructions that break language-conditioned robot policies, and finds
that state-of-the-art models fail or act unsafely on many of
them~\cite{karnik2024embodied}. LIBERO-PRO perturbs LIBERO along objects,
initial states, instructions, and environments; on the language axis it
finds that paraphrased instructions leave accuracy intact --- but so do
nonsensical ones, the policy executing the same memorized trajectory
regardless~\cite{zhou2025liberopro}. LIBERO-Plus charts the same
sensitivity across ten VLAs --- instruction perturbations cost between 14
and 50 points depending on the model --- and reaches the same verdict:
much of the apparent robustness traces to models ignoring the instruction
outright~\cite{fei2025liberoplus}. These studies report aggregate drops
over families of perturbations. Our single-edit swings are the
complementary, finer-grained observation: when the policy does attend to
language, a single word can move success by tens of points, and our oracle
search puts a number on how much of the apparent generalization gap is
phrasing.

\textbf{Training-time remedies. }
The standard fix is augmentation: relabeling demonstrations with
additional phrasings so that the policy sees many surface forms of each
instruction~\cite{kwok2026scaling}, or, as in DIAL, with VLM-generated
instructions that extend the training vocabulary~\cite{xiao2023dial}.
Steerable Policies goes further, training the VLA on synthetic commands at
several levels of abstraction --- subtasks, motions, grounded pixel
coordinates --- so that language can steer low-level
behavior~\cite{chen2026steerable}. All of these change the policy's
weights. The $\pi_0$ checkpoint we study was already trained with rephrase
augmentation~\cite{intact}; our method targets the sensitivity that
remains after such training, without training further.

\textbf{Deployment-time remedies. }
Two families leave the weights untouched. \emph{Test-time verification}
(CoVer) generates several rephrasings and several candidate actions at
every inference step and uses a contrastively trained verifier to select
the best phrase-action pair~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. It is our closest
comparison, and we evaluate against it in Section~\ref{sec:cover}; the
differences are that we act on phrasing only, never on actions, and that
we rephrase once per episode rather than at every step, so per-step
inference cost is unchanged. \emph{Instruction optimization} searches the
language input of a frozen policy: SARL learns by online reinforcement
which prompts steer a generalist policy to a given
task~\cite{bhatia2026sarl}, VLA Grounder trains an instruction-to-command
translator against rollout reward for a black-box
VLA~\cite{shodiev2026grounder}, and PALO optimizes language subtask
decompositions for few-shot adaptation~\cite{myers2024palo}. Each
optimizes per task through interaction with that task, so what it learns
does not transfer. Our rules are distilled once, offline, from eight tasks,
and apply zero-shot to instructions and tasks that contributed no evidence.

\textbf{Language as the interface between VLMs and VLAs. }
RT-2 and PaLM-E showed that web-scale vision-language pretraining
transfers to robot control~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2, driess2023palme}, and
Hi Robot places a VLM above a VLA to turn open-ended prompts into
instructions the low-level policy can follow~\cite{shi2025hirobot}. SayCan
grounds an LLM planner in learned value functions so that proposed plans
respect the robot's skills~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, and Inner Monologue
closes the loop with execution feedback~\cite{huang2022inner}. These
systems decide \emph{which} instruction to issue; ours decides how to
\emph{word} it. The rephraser is the instruction-level analogue of
affordance grounding: rather than selecting which skill to invoke, it
learns which surface forms of an instruction this particular executor
parses reliably.

\section{Models and Benchmarks}

Throughout, we work with two modern VLAs: $\pi_0$~\cite{pi0} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05}. All of our $\pi_0$ experiments are conducted on the Bridge dataset, and all of our $\pi_{0.5}$ experiments are conducted on the LIBERO dataset. Both checkpoints are inherited from prior work: $\pi_0$ finetuned on Bridge~\cite{intact}, $\pi_{0.5}$ on LIBERO~\cite{pi05}. Moreover, the finetuned version of $\pi_0$ that we use is explicitly trained on rephrases of each task in order to reduce its language sensitivity. That is, $\pi_0$ already carries a mitigation for language sensitivity; we study what remains. In our own tests, the rephrase augmentation is worth a 20\% relative improvement on natural rephrasings (28.4\% vs 23.8\% pooled success on a 192-phrase natural-rephrase set, against the otherwise-identical non-augmented checkpoint); we seek to further improve robustness with rules.

In our experiments, we measure the success of these VLAs in simulation. One design choice: at which frame is success measured? For SIMPLER's Bridge tasks, we measure success at the 60th frame of a trajectory, the benchmark's default horizon.

\subsection{Tasks and Canonical Instructions}

Each benchmark provides a set of simulatable tasks, and each task a canonical instruction.

\subsubsection{SIMPLER / Bridge}

This benchmark~\cite{simpler} provides simulated replicas of real-robot evaluation setups; we use its Bridge~\cite{bridge} suite of WidowX tabletop tasks, such as ``put carrot on plate'' and ``put eggplant into yellow basket''. We use the four Bridge tasks shipped with the benchmark and construct 16 more from benchmark-provided primitives, for 20 simulatable tasks in total. Of these, eight are in-vocabulary: every noun in the canonical instruction appears in the Bridge training language. Task success is procedurally determined by the benchmark. Our sealed evaluation set fixes 12 of the 20 ex ante --- five in-vocabulary, seven out --- and these strata are the in-distribution / out-of-distribution splits reported in our figures.

\subsubsection{LIBERO}

This benchmark~\cite{liu2023libero} consists of 130 simulatable tabletop tasks. Examples include ``put the bowl on the plate'' and ``put the moka pot on the stove''. Its tasks are grouped into four suites --- libero\_spatial, libero\_object, and libero\_goal (10 tasks each), plus libero\_100, which splits into libero\_90 (90 tasks) and libero\_10 (10). We inherit a $\pi_{0.5}$ finetuned on every suite except libero\_90~\cite{pi05}. As in SIMPLER, task success is defined procedurally by the benchmark's code. Each LIBERO task runs to a suite-specific step limit and counts as successful once any step succeeds.



\section{Characterizing a VLA's Language Sensitivity}
\label{sec:minimal-pairs}

To give a concrete sense of the language sensitivity of $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, we present \emph{single-edit swings}: sets of phrases in which a minimal edit produces a large performance swing. The sets are charted in Fig.~\ref{fig:pairs-bridge} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pairs-libero}, and the corresponding scenes are in Appendix~\ref{app:scenes}. 
Furthermore, we estimate the headroom that phrasing alone leaves a frozen VLA: if an \textit{oracle} chose the best phrase per task, how much would its success rate improve?

\subsection{Finding Single-Edit Swings}

Our search algorithm is simple: first we generate many rephrases for each task (in fact, we use the exact evidence files used by our rules' distillers). Then, with the help of an LLM, we propose sets to test, based on the evidence, and we evaluate each phrase with many attempts in simulation. After gathering success rates per phrase, we retain only those sets where the difference in accuracy between the best and worst phrase is statistically significant, that is, they are unlikely to be explained by chance (each set's phrases and exact p-values are listed in the project repository\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/vla-rephrasing-artifacts}\label{fn:repo}}). We manually filter out phrase sets with large gaps that are potentially explained by scene-based ambiguity. For $\pi_0$, we evaluate each phrase 72 times (24 layouts by 3 repetitions). For $\pi_{0.5}$, we evaluate each phrase once on each of 50 layouts. To further reduce noise in our measurements, we use common random seeds per layout / repetition pair. We note that it was qualitatively more challenging to find these phrase sets for $\pi_{0.5}$ than for $\pi_0$.

\subsection{Patterns in the Swings}

Several patterns emerge. Some swings hold across tasks and even across
models: plate $\to$ dish costs $\pi_0$ 19 points and $\pi_{0.5}$ 56, and
``hot plate'' is catastrophic wherever it appears (2\% as a source noun,
0\% as a destination). Other swings do not transfer: adding ``purple'' to
the eggplant gains 61 points on one task and costs 30 on another, so no
static rule can satisfy both. Swings were also far easier to find on
in-finetune tasks: of the 426 candidate pairs our $\pi_{0.5}$ search
evaluated, 44\% of in-finetune pairs showed a significant swing
($|\Delta| \ge 18$\,pp, $p < 0.05$), versus 5\% of out-of-finetune
pairs --- a sign that finetuned performance rests on memorized phrasings,
which a single word can break. Color shows the clearest asymmetry between
the two models: on Bridge, an added color helps on out-of-corpus nouns
(black keyboard, $+42$) and hurts on nouns the corpus knows bare (yellow
plate, $-22$); on LIBERO, an added color only ever hurts (black bowl,
$-20$). The common thread is plain naming: generic category words (can,
dish, drink, thing) lose to the specific everyday name --- even one the
training data never contained (pepsi beats soda by 39).

\subsection{Oracle Phrase Search}
\label{sec:oracle}

To further quantify language sensitivity, for each task, we compare its ``best-performing'' phrase to its canonical phrase. To find the ``best'' phrase for a task, we employ a simple algorithm. For a fixed task, we first generate 16 rephrases using a VLM. We then score each phrase by rolling it out multiple times. Then, we ask the VLM to generate 16 new phrases based on all previously scored phrases. We repeat this process three times, and select the best-performing phrase. See Fig.~\ref{fig:pi0-conditions} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pi05-fourtier} for a comparison of the success rates of oracle phrases to canonicals, adversarials, and naturals. For both models, oracle phrases reach significantly higher success rates than the other phrase sets. By this measure, $\pi_0$ is more sensitive to phrasing than $\pi_{0.5}$, consistent with our experience searching for single-edit swings.

\section{Reducing Language Sensitivity with LLM-Distilled Rephrasing Rules}

We now present our method for reducing this sensitivity. An overview of our method is given in Fig.~\ref{fig:method}. At a high level, it is quite simple: generate many rephrases across many tasks, score each phrase, present these scored phrases to an LLM, and then receive a set of rules specifying how to rephrase an incoming language instruction. Unlike per-task optimization, the rules must hold across all tasks at once.

\subsection{Gathering Evidence}

In order to derive phrasing rules, we first gather a large set of phrases. For each task in our training set, we prompt a VLM to generate 16 ``natural'' phrases --- e.g. the way a human might naturally request a task --- and 8 adversarial phrases --- e.g. a convoluted, overly complicated task request. For example, a natural rephrase of ``put carrot on plate'' is ``Could you move the carrot to the plate?'', whereas an adversarial one is ``Take the long orange item typically eaten by rabbits and ensure it ends up supported by the circular dishware.'' We also include all phrases measured during oracle search (these tend to be more benign rephrases). See the appendix for the prompt used to generate natural and adversarial phrases.

\subsubsection{Scoring Phrases}

Rule-distillation requires that each phrase gets a score, so that phrases are comparable. For simulatable tasks, we simply simulate each task / phrase pair multiple times to get a success rate. However, we also aim to measure phrases for non-simulatable tasks: these tasks are equipped with real-world expert trajectories. For these, we score rephrases using a simple metric that acts as a proxy for success rate. For each phrase, we calculate the gripper error between the ground truth action and the VLA's predicted action over 16 trajectories at 4 frames per trajectory. This gives us a set of 64 measurements which we average and use as the score. When using this proxy to measure task / phrase pairs that are simulatable, we see that their scores correlate relatively well with actual rollout success. Notably, rollout scores exist for only the eight non-sealed simulatable tasks; the twelve sealed evaluation tasks contribute no evidence.

\subsection{Distilling Rules}

To distill the evidence into a set of rules, we prompt Claude to derive rules with the prompt provided in the project repository.\footref{fn:repo} We test three rulebook types, distinguished by the evidence they see: in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both. Since rule-distillation is stochastic, we distill each type of rulebook three times, and during evaluation we average their results. In our experiments, rulebooks contain somewhere between 10 and 20 rules.

For rulebooks distilled from phrases from the finetuning dataset, we further provide the full set of finetuning phrases so that the distiller may make use of corpus statistics such as how frequently objects occur in the finetuning data, the most common phrase structures, etc. Due to cost constraints, we use Claude Fable to generate the first three rulebooks (one each of in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both), and then we repeat twice more with Claude Opus 5. Our results do not suggest this choice mattered.

All distilled rulebooks are available in the project
repository.\footref{fn:repo}

\subsection{Applying Rules}

Rephrasing rules are applied just once, before the robot's trajectory begins, by one of three appliers: Claude, Gemini, or the open-weights Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen}. Alongside the instruction and rules, the applier receives a reasoning trace generated from the incoming phrase and a scene image. These traces are generated by gemini-3.5-flash. The rules are applied greedily, and the output phrase is given directly to the VLA, after which the robot performs the task as usual.



\section{Evaluating the Rulebooks}
\label{sec:rules-results}

We evaluate $\pi_0$, finetuned on Bridge with rephrase augmentation, in simulation with SIMPLER tasks.

\subsection{Phrase Sets}
\label{sec:phrase-sets}

We evaluate on four different phrase sets: adversarials, VLM-generated naturals, human-generated naturals, and canonicals. Adversarial and VLM-Generated Natural phrases are generated by gemini-3.5-flash, given the scene image, using the prompt given in the project repository. Adversarial phrases retain the goal, but are awkwardly worded. VLM-generated naturals mimic the way a human might request a task. Human-generated naturals are gathered from friends and family via survey: the user is presented with a successful video demonstration of each task and asked to request the task of an 8-year-old, an adult, and a robot. We evaluate 6 adversarial phrases per task (72 in total), roughly 16 phrases per task for the VLM-generated naturals (186 in total), and roughly 30 phrases per task for the human-generated naturals (363 in total). We additionally evaluate the SIMPLER environment's canonical instruction accompanying each task, but we note that at just one phrase per task, one should be cautious in assigning significance to the canonical category's results (the canonical panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}).

\subsection{Baselines}

We evaluate against three baselines: no rephraser, no-rules rephraser, and CoVer~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. The no-rephraser does not rephrase at all and passes the incoming instruction directly to the VLA. The no-rules rephraser contains a single simple rule (see the project repository). CoVer uses a contrastively trained verifier to continuously select its verifier's highest-scoring phrase / action pair from a set of 40 (8 rephrases by 5 actions each). Each task is evaluated on 24 different layouts of the scene. The paired construction and permutation test behind
every reported p-value are described in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\subsection{Results}

Our best-performing rulebook type is consistently the out-of-finetune rulebook (the exception is the canonical set, where the in-finetune rulebook performs best; however, we note that at just a single phrase per task, our evaluation of canonical phrases is statistically underpowered). The gains concentrate on out-of-distribution tasks. With the out-of-finetune rulebooks, the Claude applier increases performance, on average, by more than 20\% across adversarial and natural phrases when compared to the no-rephraser baseline. When compared to the no-rules rephraser baseline, it improves performance by 26\% on adversarials, 11\% on human-generated naturals, and 4.7\% on VLM-generated naturals. See Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard} and Tables~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline} and~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold} for the detailed set of results. Across the three independent draws of a rulebook type, pooled success varies by 0.7--5.1\,pp (median range 3.0\,pp) --- small relative to the effects above.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Much remains. Rulebooks distilled from more tasks may yield larger
gains, and a more formal reinforcement-learning treatment of phrasing is
appealing, though it would likely demand far more simulatable tasks than
we worked with. Our in-finetune rulebooks underperformed, possibly
because of a weak proxy score; a cheaper, better proxy would avoid the
cost of full rollouts. We evaluated the method on a single VLA and only
in simulation; other VLAs and real robots are natural next steps. Most
importantly, language sensitivity should be measured systematically
across models. Explaining it --- attributing the sensitivity to a
cause --- would be the most exciting outcome of all.

\section{Statistical Tests}
\label{app:stats}

For a comparison between two arms, the paired unit is the \emph{base
instruction}: $d_i = c_i - b_i$, the two arms' success rates on base $i$
over the same pinned layout grid (rulebook arms average their three draws
before pairing). Common random numbers make the two arms face identical
scenes, so scene difficulty cancels within each $d_i$. The reported
p-value is a two-sided sign-flip permutation test on the mean of the
$d_i$ (20{,}000 resamples; exact enumeration when $n \le 12$). The number
of bases is the sample size ($n = 186$ VLM-generated natural, $363$
human-generated natural, $72$ adversarial, $12$ canonical); layouts and
repetitions sharpen each $d_i$ but do not add to $n$.
Tables~\ref{tab:pscores-baseline} and~\ref{tab:pscores-scaffold} report
each effect as a relative change: the mean paired difference as a percent
of the baseline's pooled rate (adversarial 24.5\%, human-generated
naturals 23.0\%, LLM-generated naturals 26.0\%; scaffold rates per
applier and condition).
