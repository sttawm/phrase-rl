\begin{abstract}

Much progress has been made towards building general purpose robots. These robots are capable of being instructed by humans via natural language to perform a wide variety of tasks. However, many of the state of the art, open robots, implemented as Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs), are still very sensitive to instruction phrasing: variation in task phrasing can change task success rates by more than 60 percentage points. We characterize this gap in detail on a state-of-the-art open VLA. We then compare three different methods of learning a given VLA's preferred language. We package these learnings into a "rephraser" that sits between the human and the robot, translating each instruction into the VLA's preferred phrasing. Our best method raises VLA task success on adversarially phrased instructions from 26.6\% to 31.6\%, and on naturally phrased instructions from 28.5\% to 36.9\% --- fully recovering natural-phrasing damage, to the level the executor achieves on its own original instructions (36.1\%).

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We have come a long way toward developing general-purpose robots -- implemented as Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) -- that can be instructed via natural language to perform a wide variety of tasks. These robots are built on top of Large Language Models (LLMs) which themselves demonstrate a super-human command of language. However, there's something lacking in the way that LLM's language skills transfer to VLAs: \sout{modern VLAs} $\pi_0$ is extremely sensitive to the specific phrasing of its given natural language instruction. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pi0_conditions}, changes to task phrasing can affect \sout{VLA} $\pi_0$ performance by more than 60 percentage points; see Fig.~\ref{fig:spread_coke_can_on_keyboard}, ~\ref{fig:spread_cube_on_plate}, and ~\ref{fig:spread_eggplant_on_keyboard} for examples. Despite the fact that a VLA may have the required motor skills to perform a task, it is often not straightforward to find the optimal language instruction to trigger the skill.

In an attempt to address these issues we add a \textit{translator} model that translates incoming task-instructions into (what is ideally) the robot's preferred form. We try three different methods. Each of our methods inspects the robot's training data and optionally some simulated rollouts of various task rephrasings.

Our first and most successful method we call \textit{LLM-derived rules}. In this method, we first calculate VLA task success across many different task phrasings. We perform this phrase-comparison for both its real-world training set and in a simulated environment. Since we cannot directly measure task success on the robot's real-world training data, we use a gripper-based proxy. This proxy shows high correlation with actual task success, especially when sampled at many different frames along a robot's task trajectory. After gathering a dataset of per-task phrase-rankings, we ask an LLM to derive observations about how phrasing affects task success. These observations are compiled into rules, and the rules are given in a prompt to an off-the-shelf VLM (Vision Language Model) rephraser. This is the method reported in the abstract; it raises VLA task success on adversarially phrased instructions from 26.6\% to 31.6\% and restores naturally phrased instructions from 28.5\% to 36.9\% --- level with the 36.1\% the executor achieves on its original instructions. A variant that additionally mines the executor's training data deepens adversarial repair to 34.3\% at some cost on natural inputs (\S\ref{sec:results-comparison}).

Our second method is more or less the "weights" based, RL counterpart to the above. In this method, we test many rephrasing per task, ranking the results according to our gripper-based proxy reward and apply GRPO. We have yet to see significant improvement when using this method, though it does seem that the proxy reward is able to be mildly optimized. 

Our third method trains a VLM to map rephrased training tasks back to their original training task. In our evaluation, it helps with in-vocabulary tasks and hurts on out-of-vocabulary tasks. For future work, it may be interesting to endow this method with access to our proxy-reward function; on roughly a third of evaluated training tasks (69 of 213), our proxy-guided search found a phrasing that outscored the original.







\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language-Sensitivity. }
Other works call attention to the language-sensitivity of language-conditioned robot policies ~\cite{karnik2024embodied}. 

\textbf{Reducing Language Sensitivity. }
Recent works improve VLA performance by augmenting its training data with additional rephrases of its training instructions ~\cite{kwok2026scaling, chen2026steerable}.

In conjunction with the above, a frozen VLA's performance can be improved by generating many phrase-action pairs at runtime and then using a pre-trained model to select the best candidate ~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. This method is our closest known comparison, yet we operate solely on task-phrasing; that is, we do not explicitly select good candidate actions. Moreover, though generating multiple phrase-action pairs at each inference step is relatively inexpensive, we avoid this cost by generating our rephrase just once, at the beginning of the episode.  

\textbf{Using a VLM to improve VLA performance. }
Another body of work incorporates a VLM in some capacity to improve the performance of a downstream VLA \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme,shi2025hirobot}.

\textbf{Accounting for robot capabilities. }
A complementary line of work conditions the \emph{language} side of a
system on what the robot can actually do: SayCan grounds an LLM planner in
learned value functions so that proposed plans respect the robot's
skills~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, and Inner Monologue closes the loop with
execution feedback~\cite{huang2022inner}. Our rephraser is the
instruction-level analogue: rather than selecting \emph{which} skill to
invoke, it learns which \emph{surface forms} of an instruction this
executor parses reliably, and edits the instruction accordingly.



\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Vision-Language-Action models are highly sensitive to the phrasing of
their instructions, even when task semantics are unchanged. We propose
inserting a \emph{rephraser} between the user and the executor
(Fig.~\ref{fig:deployment}) that translates each incoming instruction
into the executor's preferred phrasing, leaving the executor itself
frozen. We first build the instruments for characterizing phrase
sensitivity (\S\ref{sec:characterizing}) and select a reward function
for scoring candidate phrasings (\S\ref{sec:reward}); we then present
three methods of increasing training commitment for learning the
rephraser (Fig.~\ref{fig:training_flow_compact}).

\subsection{Characterizing Phrase Sensitivity}
\label{sec:characterizing}

\textbf{Oracle Phrase Search.}
To upper-bound what phrasing alone can contribute, we search for each
task's best-performing instruction: a pool of candidate rewordings
(LLM-proposed paraphrases and systematic variants of the original
instruction) is rolled in simulation, and the candidate with the
highest success rate on a selection split of scene layouts is retained
as that task's \emph{oracle phrasing}. To limit selection bias, oracle
phrases are selected on 18 of the 24 evaluation layouts and reported on
all 24. The gap between oracle and original phrasings measures the headroom
available to any rephrasing method.

\textbf{Adversarial Phrase Generation.}
To lower-bound robustness, we generate adversarial rewordings that
preserve the task goal while avoiding the executor's preferred surface
forms: objects are referenced by description or indirection rather than
their canonical names (e.g.\ ``the plastic-handled eating utensil'' for
a spoon), with scene-plausible attributes added. Rewordings are
authored by an image-conditioned LLM (Gemini,
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash}, temperature 0.8), seeded with four few-shot
examples of the genre from the INT-ACT release~\cite{intact}; the full
prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{app:ert-prompt}. Importantly, the
adversarial generator used for \emph{evaluation} is distinct from the
model that generates hostile variants for \emph{training}
(\S\ref{sec:sft}), so evaluation attacks are held out from everything
the learned methods see.

\textbf{Natural Phrase Generation.}
Between these extremes, we measure robustness to the phrasing variation
real users produce: $K{=}16$ rephrases of each instruction, generated
by an image-conditioned VLM (Gemini Pro, \texttt{gemini-pro-latest})
prompted for natural, everyday phrasing with no knowledge of the
executor, its training data, or any phrasing heuristics
(Appendix~\ref{app:natural-prompt}). Because the generator sees the
scene, natural rephrases include perceptual re-descriptions (``the blue
cloth'' for a towel) --- a qualitatively different stress than
text-only paraphrasing, and the one deployed systems actually face.

\subsection{Selecting a Reward Function}
\label{sec:reward}

Both the phrase search (\S\ref{sec:search-rules}) and RL
(\S\ref{sec:grpo}) require scoring a candidate phrase by how well the
executor performs when conditioned on it, in two regimes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:training_flow_compact}).

\textbf{Simulated success-rate.}
Where a task is simulatable, the score is the empirical success rate of
the frozen executor conditioned on the candidate phrase, rolled over a
fixed set of scene layouts. This is the ground-truth signal, but it is
expensive and unavailable for tasks that exist only as real-world
demonstrations.

\textbf{A gripper-error proxy from ground-truth trajectories.}
For the non-simulatable regime we score a phrase by how well the
phrase-conditioned executor reconstructs recorded expert trajectories:
at $F$ sampled frames of $C$ demonstration episodes, we query the
frozen executor and measure the error of its predicted
\emph{gripper-channel} actions against the ground truth --- whether the
phrase triggers correct grasp/release behavior at the correct moments.
We also train an ensemble of learned \emph{verifiers} over the
executor's full predicted-action features; alone it is equal to or worse
than the raw gripper signal at practical budgets
(Fig.~\ref{fig:reward_grids_gripper}). Our final reward is a logistic
success model fitted on 254 phrases with rollout-measured success,
combining both channels into a calibrated success estimate.

\textbf{Validation and budget allocation.}
We validated the proxy on four simulatable tasks. For two, real
training demonstrations exist and the training instruction applies
verbatim in simulation (``put the spoon on the towel'', ``put the
green cube on top of the yellow cube''); for the other two, we
generated demonstrations by keeping successful simulated episodes.
In all four, the simulator measures each phrase's true success rate,
while the proxy scores the same phrases by comparing the executor's
predicted actions against the demonstrated actions --- and we count how often the two agree on which of two phrases is
better: 92.4\% on clearly-different pairs, and 79.6\% when true
success differs by only 5--10 points ($F{=}4$, $C{=}20$). More episodes beat more frames
at equal cost, so the search screens cheaply ($F{=}1$, $C{=}4$) and
re-scores finalists carefully ($F{=}4$, $C{=}10$), while RL accepts
noisier scores for throughput ($C{=}5$, $F{=}4$) --- group averaging
washes the noise out.

\subsection{Scene-Description Traces}
\label{sec:traces}

All three methods condition the rephraser on a \emph{trace}: a short
scene description, plus a reading of the instruction in the context of
the scene, written by an image-conditioned VLM (Gemini,
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash}) from the robot's initial camera frame. Traces are generated from the \emph{incoming} phrase,
never from the task's original instruction --- a trace derived from the
original would leak the executor's canonical vocabulary into exactly the
degraded inputs whose repair we want to measure. The trace is the only
scene channel our text-only rephrasers see: it supplies the visible
object names for rule application (\S\ref{sec:search-rules}), the
context half of SFT training pairs (\S\ref{sec:sft}), and the
conditioning for RL sampling (\S\ref{sec:grpo}).

\subsection{Reward-Guided Phrase Search and LLM Rule Distillation}
\label{sec:search-rules}

This method runs the loop of Fig.~\ref{fig:training_flow_compact} twice,
producing two successive rule sets that we evaluate separately. The first
iteration reuses the oracle search of \S\ref{sec:characterizing} on
simulatable tasks, scoring candidates by simulated success-rate, and
distills the scored phrases into \emph{rollout-derived} rules. The
second iteration searches the \emph{training} corpus --- where no
simulator is available --- scoring candidates with the trajectory proxy of
\S\ref{sec:reward}, with the rollout-derived rules informing candidate
authoring; its results are distilled, together with the rollout-derived
rules and corpus statistics (vocabulary counts over the training
instructions), into the \emph{rollout{+}training-data} rules. The two turn out to occupy
different points on a repair--conservatism trade-off
(\S\ref{sec:results-comparison}).

\textbf{Candidates and task selection.}
Candidate rephrasings are authored by an image-conditioned LLM from the
instruction and a scene frame, guided by the first iteration's rules
and style examples from the oracle search (prompt in
Appendix~\ref{app:search-prompts}); the original is always retained as
a candidate. Search budget goes to the 213 training instructions with
the most recorded episodes --- selecting for score reliability rather
than predicted headroom.

\textbf{Search.}
Within a task, every candidate is scored on the same episodes and
frames and ranked by reward. Eight authored candidates plus the
original are screened cheaply ($F{=}1$, $C{=}4$); the top three are
re-scored with the original at the finals budget ($F{=}4$, $C{=}10$).
The winner then seeds up to two hill-climbing rounds: an LLM proposes
eight variations of the incumbent, all are scored on identical frames,
and a variation is adopted only if it beats the incumbent's
simultaneous re-score by a minimum margin. The output is a per-task
ranking of phrases with their margins over the original.

\textbf{Rule distillation with hypothesis gating.}
An LLM reads the accumulated rankings --- winners, losers, and margins
across tasks --- together with the previous rule set, and proposes
updated rules for how phrasing affects this executor. Proposed rules
are treated as hypotheses: each is tested by applying it to held-out
training instructions and scoring the rewrites with the same reward.
This gate proved decisive. Of 817 elite searched rewrites, only 20.6\%
beat their original, and all 14 candidate \emph{improvement} rules
failed held-out testing (mean $-1.8$\,pp per rule-following rewrite).
The distillation therefore converged on a conservative protocol: the
final rules primarily teach the rephraser to \emph{repair} defective
inputs --- restoring canonical object nouns and training-register
phrasing --- and to leave already-canonical inputs untouched. The full rule sets are released with our code.

\textbf{Automation.}
We have since packaged this pipeline as an automated loop: each iteration
rephrases a fixed evaluation set with the current rules, scores the
rewrites (proxy on training tasks, simulated rollouts on simulatable
ones), and passes the accumulated evidence to the distiller together with
an LLM judge's report on whether the rewrites actually \emph{followed}
the rules. The results reported here come from the supervised runs
described above; the automated loop is how we continue iterating.

\textbf{Deployment.}
The frozen rule set is placed in the prompt of an off-the-shelf VLM,
which rewrites each incoming instruction once, at the start of the
episode (Fig.~\ref{fig:deployment}).

\subsection{Supervised Finetuning (SFT) from Rephrases to Canonical Instructions}
\label{sec:sft}
Our second method learns \emph{back-translation}: mapping degraded
instructions back to the executor's preferred form. For each of 17{,}297
unique Bridge training instructions we author three hostile variants with
the \emph{frozen} rephraser model itself (synonym and attribute
injection, indirect reference, terse compression), add benign paraphrase
pairs, and finetune Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen} with LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora}
on the resulting 73k variant$\rightarrow$original pairs (loss on the
target only; no checkpoint selection). The strongest variant prepends a
scene trace (\S\ref{sec:traces}); for cost reasons SFT uses traces
self-generated by the same Qwen model rather than a commercial VLM.
Because training attacks are authored by Qwen while evaluation attacks
are authored by Gemini (\S\ref{sec:characterizing}), the evaluation
attack distribution is held out from training. 

\subsection{Learning to Rephrase with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)}
\label{sec:grpo}
Our third method trains the rephraser against the reward directly with
GRPO~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath}: the policy (Qwen3.5-9B with
LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora}) samples $K{=}16$ rephrases of each incoming
instruction, each candidate is scored by the frozen executor
($F{=}4$, $C{=}5$), and group-normalized advantages update the policy
under a KL leash ($\beta{=}0.15$) to the frozen base, without which the
policy collapses. Around this skeleton we varied most of the recipe
across runs: the conditioning (bare instruction, scene-trace
conditioned, and image-bearing prompts); the mix of original, natural,
and adversarial training inputs (1:2:1 and 1:1:2); the reward
(gripper-only, gripper/verifier rank blends, and --- in early runs ---
action-distance scores that proved uncorrelated with rollout success and
were abandoned); and the update rule (GRPO over the full sampled group
versus advantage-weighted finetuning restricted to top-ranked candidate
sets, with and without replay). \S\ref{sec:results-comparison} reports
the common outcome. The next revision replaces within-group ranks with
the calibrated success model of \S\ref{sec:reward} --- a magnitude
cannot amplify scoring noise into gradient the way a rank can --- and
restores image conditioning. 


\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Executors and simulation.}
We evaluate on the SIMPLER suite~\cite{simpler} of WidowX manipulation
tasks in simulation, using the INT-ACT evaluation
stack~\cite{intact}. Our executors are two frozen $\pi_0$
policies~\cite{pi0} fine-tuned on Bridge data~\cite{bridge}, both from
the INT-ACT release~\cite{intact}: a standard fine-tune and a
\emph{rephrase-augmented} fine-tune trained with paraphrase augmentation.
Unless noted otherwise, sealed results use the rephrase-augmented
executor. Neither executor is updated by any of our methods; all learning
acts on the instruction channel alone.

\textbf{Sealed evaluation suite.}
Our primary benchmark is a \emph{sealed} test suite of 12 tasks
$\times$ 24 scene layouts, frozen before method development and never
used for training, tuning, or prompt iteration. Each evaluation run
rolls 12 episodes per (task, layout) cell, i.e.\ 3{,}456 episodes. All
runs were preregistered before execution; every phrase set was inspected
before its first exposure to the sealed suite.

\textbf{Vocabulary strata.}
We stratify the 12 sealed tasks by whether their key object nouns occur
in the executor's training corpus: 5 tasks are \emph{in-vocabulary} and
7 are \emph{out-of-vocabulary}. We report pooled success alongside both
strata, since phrasing interventions behave differently when the
canonical noun is unavailable to the executor.

\textbf{Adversarial and natural conditions.}
The adversarial condition rolls one adversarial rewording per task
(\S\ref{sec:characterizing}) over the full grid: 12 instructions
$\times$ 24 layouts $\times$ 12 repetitions (3{,}456 episodes). The
natural condition rolls all $K{=}16$ natural rephrases per task, one
episode per (rephrase, layout), over all 24 layouts (4{,}608 episodes).

\textbf{Development and validation sets.}
Method development, reward-function selection, and RL monitoring use
separate training tasks and a fixed validation probe (8 tasks $\times$
24 layouts, one episode each); no sealed task--layout cell is ever used
for development.

\textbf{Metrics.}
We report task success rate (\%) under SIMPLER's per-task success
criteria, pooled over tasks and stratified by vocabulary. At
$n=3{,}456$ episodes per sealed run, the standard error of a pooled
rate near 35\% is ${\sim}0.8$ percentage points.

\subsection{How Phrase-Sensitive Are VLAs?}
\label{sec:results-sensitivity}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Sealed-suite success (\%) by input condition and vocabulary
stratum (12 tasks $\times$ 24 layouts $\times$ 12 reps per condition;
pooled SE ${\sim}0.8$\,pp). No rephraser is applied.}
\label{tab:sensitivity}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
Input condition & Pooled & In-vocab (5) & OOV (7) \\
\hline
Oracle phrasings & \textbf{48.2} & 50.3 & 46.8 \\
Original instructions & 36.1 & 48.3 & 27.3 \\
Natural rephrases ($K{=}16$) & 28.5 & 41.5 & 19.2 \\
Adversarial (ERT) & 26.6 & 30.6 & 23.7 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} characterizes phrase sensitivity on the
sealed suite. Three observations drive everything that follows.

\textbf{Headroom is large, and concentrated out-of-vocabulary.}
Oracle phrasings add 12.2 points over the original instructions. The
originals show a 21-point cliff between in-vocab and OOV tasks (48.3 vs
27.3); the oracle nearly erases it (50.3 vs 46.8) --- when a task's key
noun is missing from the executor's training vocabulary, almost the
entire deficit is recoverable by rewording alone. Individual tasks are
dramatic: ``put carrot on ramekin'' scores 14.6\% while ``pick up the
carrot and place it in the white container'' scores 50.0\% ($n{=}288$
each); ``put coke can on wheel'' 12.2\% becomes 45.5\% as ``put coke
can in black wheel''; and deleting one word (``put coke can on
keyboard'' $\rightarrow$ ``put coke on keyboard'') is worth $+11$
points.

\textbf{Natural rephrasing costs three quarters of what an adversary
costs.} Naturally phrased requests --- the input deployed systems
actually face --- lose 7.6 points against the originals; deliberately
hostile rewordings lose 9.5. On OOV tasks naturals are the \emph{worst}
condition measured (19.2, below even adversarial 23.7), and an everyday
request like ``Move the carrot to the ramekin.''\ can score 0/24 on a
task whose original scores ${\sim}28$.
Figs.~\ref{fig:spread_coke_can_on_keyboard}--\ref{fig:spread_eggplant_on_keyboard}
show per-phrase spreads: on sensitive OOV tasks, near-synonymous requests
span 0--58 points.

\textbf{Random rephrasing has no headroom.} Sixteen unguided rephrases
of each original average 29.5 --- \emph{below} the originals --- and
even selecting the best of the 16 per task (37.8) barely clears them
(36.1). The oracle gap is reachable only by \emph{informed} search:
knowing what this executor prefers is worth 12 points; guessing is worth
nothing.

\subsection{Comparing the Three Learning Methods}
\label{sec:results-comparison}

All three methods are evaluated as drop-in rephrasers in front of the
frozen executor (Fig.~\ref{fig:deployment}), against the conditions of
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}. In summary: the rules method repairs both
degraded conditions and is the only method whose gains survive
out-of-vocabulary; SFT redistributes success toward in-vocab tasks
without net gain; and GRPO optimizes its reward without moving rollout
success at all.

\subsubsection{LLM-distilled rules}
On \textbf{natural} inputs (Fig.~\ref{fig:natural_ladder}), the
rollout-derived rules applied by Gemini restore pooled success to 36.9\% --- above the 36.1\%
original-instruction reference, i.e.\ complete repair of
natural-phrasing damage ($+8.4$ over passthrough 28.5). The same frozen
applier \emph{without} the rules reaches 31.2: most of the value is the
distilled knowledge, not the rewriting capacity. On \textbf{adversarial}
inputs (Fig.~\ref{fig:sealed_ladder_adv}) the rule sets trade places:
the rollout{+}training-data rules deepen repair to 33.3 (Gemini) and
34.3 (Claude; $+7.7$ over passthrough, the deepest adversarial repair we
measured) versus 31.6 for the rollout-derived rules. On unmodified
\textbf{original} instructions the best configuration does no harm: the
rollout{+}training-data rules applied by Claude score 37.7, slightly
above the originals themselves. Gains are OOV-concentrated throughout
--- on original instructions the rollout{+}training-data rules reach OOV
30.9 vs 27.3 passthrough, while in-vocab gives back two points (46.0 vs
48.3) --- the pattern that motivates the in-distribution gate discussed
in \S\ref{sec:results-analysis}.

\S\ref{sec:results-analysis} returns to what this implies about the
proxy.

\subsubsection{GRPO}
\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{v11_nat24.png}
    \caption{RL does not move ground truth: rollout success of the
GRPO policy's rewrites on 192-phrase natural probes over 240 training
steps (val-8 tasks). Drift $-0.8$\,pp, within the $\pm7.1$\,pp noise
band; across 25 cells the trend is $-0.005$\,pp/step (95\% CI
$[-0.013, +0.002]$). Over the same steps the policy closed 17.7\% of
its reward gap to the original instructions.}
    \label{fig:v11_nat24}
\end{figure}

The GRPO recipe of \S\ref{sec:grpo} reliably climbs its reward and
just as reliably fails to move rollout success
(Fig.~\ref{fig:v11_nat24}). The decomposition is diagnostic: nearly all
reward movement came through the learned-verifier channel
($+0.55$--$0.85$) while the gripper channel moved $0.005$; the
calibrated success model's own coefficients predict $+6$\,pp from such
a verifier gain, and the measured change is $-1.1\pm1.3$\,pp. Given
gradient access, the policy found the reward's exploitable channel and
optimized it off the data manifold. Candidate diversity also collapsed
(median 6/16 unique samples; the frozen model at the same temperature
yields 14.8/16, so the collapse is trained, not a decoding artifact) ---
but a sibling run with $3\times$ better diversity was equally flat, so
collapse does not explain the null. An earlier recipe variant that
\emph{did} move showed the second failure mode: adversarial-input
repair rose from 34.5\% to 39.1\% by step 120, then declined below
baseline (34.9\% by step 200) as training continued --- an inverted U
whose peak checkpoint is the only RL artifact that transferred anything.

\subsubsection{SFT}
On development tasks SFT looks like a win: $+5.3\pm2.3$ pooled over its
frozen twin, with the best in-vocab scores we recorded (56.3 vs 38.5).
The sealed suite exposes the mechanism: against the matched no-finetune
baseline, SFT moves in-vocab tasks up and OOV tasks down by roughly
$\pm15$ points each, netting $+0.9$ (CI $[-2.7,+4.2]$) --- and lands
\emph{below} doing nothing at all (24.6 vs 26.6 passthrough pooled).
Trained to canonicalize into training vocabulary, it destroys exactly
the OOV nouns that matter: ``ramekin'' becomes ``the white object''
(3.8\%) where the visual rename ``white bowl'' scores 64\%. Scene
traces partially rescue this in development; the redistribution
survives.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:results-analysis}

\textbf{Rules beat frozen rephrasers; how much the applier matters
depends on the rules.}
An off-the-shelf VLM asked to rewrite instructions \emph{without} any
distilled knowledge already helps slightly on degraded inputs (bare
Gemini: 27.8 vs.\ 26.6 passthrough on adversarial, 31.2 vs.\ 28.5 on
naturals). The rules multiply that --- $+5.5$ further points on
adversarial and $+5.7$ on naturals for the same frozen applier --- so
the distilled knowledge, not the rewriting capacity, carries most of the
value. The two rule sets then part ways on \emph{who} can spend that
knowledge. The rollout-derived rules are applier-robust: on adversarial
inputs all three appliers land within 0.6 points of one another (31.0 to
31.6 --- frozen Qwen matches Claude), and on naturals both capable
appliers fully repair (Gemini 36.9, Claude 36.4). The
rollout{+}training-data rules are applier-\emph{sensitive}: their
deeper adversarial repair exists only in capable hands (Claude 34.3 /
Gemini 33.3 / frozen Qwen 30.1), on naturals they open a 2.7-point gap
between the same two appliers the simpler rules held together (33.9 vs
31.2), and for the weakest applier they are outright harmful --- a
license to rewrite without the capacity to comply: frozen Qwen with the
full rulebook scores 27.8 on unmodified original instructions, 8.3
points below leaving them untouched. Prompt \emph{architecture} shows
the same capability gradient: swapping a scaffolded template for a bare
one is worth $+5.4$ to Qwen, $\pm0$ to Gemini, and $-1.2$ to Claude.
The pattern is consistent: the more a rulebook asks of its applier, the
more the applier's capability becomes the binding constraint.

\textbf{An in-distribution gate is the cheapest missing piece.}
Every intervention pays a small in-vocab tax (rules: $-2.3$ on
original-instruction in-vocab tasks; SFT: its entire OOV loss) while earning its keep
out-of-vocabulary and on degraded inputs. A classifier that recognizes
already-canonical, in-vocabulary instructions and passes them through
untouched would keep the OOV gains at essentially no cost; none of our
methods currently has one.

\textbf{What did the trajectory proxy contribute?}
Less than we expected, and the evidence is worth stating carefully. The
rollout{+}training-data rules' advantage is confined to adversarial
inputs ($+1.7$ to $+3.3$); on naturals the rollout-derived rules win for
every applier
(Fig.~\ref{fig:a28}). Moreover the training-data evidence bundles
proxy-\emph{scored} search results with plain corpus statistics
(vocabulary counts), and the distillation's own hypothesis gate rejected
all 14 proxy-motivated improvement rules while keeping repair rules ---
so rollout evidence carries most of the naturals value, and the
training-data pass mainly contributed conservatism: helpful under
attack, costly on benign variation, with the proxy-vs-corpus-statistics
split not yet isolated. The RL result sharpens the caution: given
gradient access, a policy optimized the proxy's exploitable channel
rather than its transferable one. Distillation into human-readable
rules, gated on held-out measurement, appears to be the safer consumer
of a noisy reward.

\section{Conclusions}

A frozen $\pi_0$ executor's success rate depends heavily on instruction
surface form: 12 points of oracle headroom, most of it on
out-of-vocabulary tasks, and a 7.6-point penalty for the natural
phrasing variation real users produce. Of the three ways we tried to
learn a rephraser, the LLM rule-distillation loop generalized best ---
fully repairing natural-phrasing damage and most adversarial damage ---
while SFT traded out-of-vocabulary performance for in-vocabulary gains
and GRPO optimized a reward channel that did not transfer. That an
explicit, human-readable rulebook out-generalized both weight-updating
methods is, to us, the interesting result: the transferable knowledge
was small and portable, and the methods that tried to bake it into
weights overfit either the vocabulary (SFT) or the reward (GRPO).
Whether weights-based methods can be made to generalize the way the
rulebook does --- or whether the rulebook's legibility and its held-out
hypothesis gate are precisely what protected it --- is the question this
comparison leaves open.

Future work:
\begin{itemize}
\item a weights-based RL recipe that transfers (our revised
calibrated-reward recipe of \S\ref{sec:grpo} is specified and queued);
\item an in-distribution classifier gating the rephraser
(\S\ref{sec:results-analysis}), which would keep out-of-vocabulary
gains without the in-vocab tax;
\item more simulation coverage, reducing reliance on the trajectory
proxy for both RL and rule evidence;
\item longer, fully automated runs of the rules loop --- the results
here come from a few supervised iterations;
\item sandboxing the rule-derivation loop so that sealed evaluation
tasks are provably invisible to every stage of it;
\item other executors and datasets; and
\item applying the same machinery one level up, to plan phrasing rather
than instruction phrasing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix}
\section{Natural-Rephrase Generation Prompt}
\label{app:natural-prompt}

Natural rephrases are generated with Gemini Pro
(\texttt{gemini-pro-latest}, accessed August 2026) at temperature 1.0,
two sampled calls of 8 rephrases per instruction, deduplicated
(case-insensitive) to $K{=}16$. The scene image is attached to each
call. The prompt deliberately contains no information about the
executor, its training data, or any phrasing heuristics; the only
register target is natural human phrasing.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent\textit{System prompt:}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are a text-transformation assistant for robot manipulation tasks.\\[3pt]
You will be given:\\
- An image of the scene.\\
- An instruction describing a manipulation goal.\\[3pt]
Your task is to:\\
1. Understand the meaning of the original instruction.\\
2. Reword the instruction into multiple alternatives that preserve the
original intent.\\[3pt]
Guidelines:\\
- Every reworded instruction must be something a person might actually
say when asking for this task to be done.\\
- All reworded instructions must mean the same thing as the original.
\end{quote}

\noindent\textit{User prompt (per instruction, image attached):}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Given the original instruction: "\{src\}", and the attached image,
generate 8 reworded instructions that convey the same objective.\\[3pt]
Guidelines for rephrasing:\\
1. Write the way real people actually talk when asking for a task ---
natural, everyday phrasing.\\
2. Different people phrase the same request differently: vary the
phrasing the way different natural speakers would.\\
3. Each rephrase must keep the same objects and the same goal as the
original.\\
4. You may refer to objects the way they appear in the image, but do
not contradict the original instruction or mention things you cannot
see.\\[3pt]
Format your response as (no analysis, no commentary):\\
Reworded Instructions:\\
1. <Alternative phrasing 1>\\
...\\[3pt]
Original Instruction:\\
\{src\}
\end{quote}

\section{Adversarial Instruction Generation Prompt}
\label{app:ert-prompt}

Sealed adversarial instructions are authored by Gemini
(\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash}, temperature 0.8), one per task, with the
scene image attached. \texttt{\{shots\}} is filled with four
\mbox{original $\rightarrow$ ERT} example pairs taken verbatim from the
INT-ACT release's extreme-rephrasing examples~\cite{intact};
\texttt{\{nominal\}} is the task's original instruction. The first line
of the response is kept.

\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are building an Extreme Rephrasing Test (ERT) for robot
instructions: adversarial rewordings that keep the task goal but are
maximally hostile to a language-conditioned robot policy. The attached
image shows the actual scene. Reference objects by description or
indirect means rather than their plain names, add scene-plausible
attributes, keep the SAME goal. Examples of the genre:\\
\{shots\}\\[3pt]
Nominal instruction: "\{nominal\}"\\
Produce exactly ONE complete ERT rephrasing (output just the
instruction, no quotes, no explanation).
\end{quote}

\section{Phrase-Search Prompts}
\label{app:search-prompts}

\noindent\textit{Candidate generation (one call per instruction, scene
image attached; \{shots\} holds three style examples from the val-set
oracle search):}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are helping phrase instructions for a robot arm policy trained on
kitchen-manipulation demonstrations (BridgeData). The attached image
shows the robot's actual camera view for this task.\\
Current instruction: "\{nominal\}"\\
Write 8 alternative phrasings the policy is likely to follow RELIABLY.
Guidelines learned from prior experiments:\\
- Name objects with common household words the training data would
contain; if an object's name is unusual, rename it to what it looks
like (e.g. "ramekin" -> "white bowl").\\
- Add concrete color/appearance adjectives ONLY for objects visible in
the image.\\
- Keep the imperative form and the same task meaning. Keep each under
15 words.\\
- Vary structure across the 8 (some minimal edits, some full
rewrites).\\
Style examples of phrasings that performed well elsewhere:\\
\{shots\}\\
Output exactly 8 lines, one phrasing per line, no numbering, no quotes.
\end{quote}

\noindent\textit{Hill-climb variation (per round, image attached):}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
The attached image is a robot arm's camera view. The current
best-performing instruction phrasing for this scene is:\\[2pt]
"\{seed\}"\\[2pt]
Write \{k\} close VARIATIONS of this phrasing that might work even
better for a robot policy trained on kitchen-manipulation
demonstrations: keep its winning structure but vary object adjectives,
object names (common household words, matching what is visible), and
verb choice. Keep each under 15 words, imperative form, same task
meaning.\\
Output exactly \{k\} lines, one per line, no numbering, no quotes.
\end{quote}
