\section{Introduction}

Humans have long dreamed of having general-purpose robots for help with everyday physical tasks. In recent years, huge advancements have been made toward developing generalist, large language and vision models (VLMs) to help with language and vision tasks. It's no surprise, then, that we have been aiming to build our generalist, physical robots --- vision-language-action models (VLAs) --- as extensions of our modern VLMs, hoping to leverage and extend their capabilities. But do VLAs fully utilize the capabilities of their underlying VLMs? We notice that language-conditioned robots (or, at least, those involved in our experiments) can still be very sensitive to variations in phrasing. And this is contrary to what one might expect after using a modern language model.

While we do not yet go so far as to identify the cause, we (1) aim to demonstrate this language sensitivity, and we (2) propose a relatively simple yet effective technique for reducing the language-sensitivity of a robot after observing its performance over a relatively small set of tasks. To reduce language-sensitivity, we measure the robot's performance across many phrasings of a small set of tasks, compiling the result into an evidence file, and then we ask a frontier LLM to generate a set of ``rephrasing rules'' based on the evidence. Then, when the VLA is given a language instruction, we pass the instruction, scene image, and rules to a rephraser which rephrases according to the rules.

We evaluate in simulation on a variety of phrasing categories --- awkward, adversarially phrased instructions, naturally-phrased instructions generated by a VLM, and naturally-phrased instructions written by humans --- and see resulting performance improvements between 16\% and 27\% for our best type of rulebook when compared to our baseline $\pi_0$ model evaluated on SIMPLER tasks for the Bridge dataset.




\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language-Sensitivity. }
Other works call attention to the language-sensitivity of language-conditioned robot policies ~\cite{karnik2024embodied}.

\textbf{Reducing Language Sensitivity. }
Recent works improve VLA performance by augmenting its training data with additional rephrases of its training instructions ~\cite{kwok2026scaling, chen2026steerable}.

In conjunction with the above, a frozen VLA's performance can be improved by generating many phrase-action pairs at runtime and then using a pre-trained model to select the best candidate ~\cite{kwok2026scaling}. This method is our closest known comparison, yet we operate solely on task-phrasing; that is, we do not explicitly select good candidate actions. Moreover, though generating multiple phrase-action pairs at each inference step is relatively inexpensive, we avoid this cost by generating our rephrase just once, at the beginning of the episode.

\textbf{Using a VLM to improve VLA performance. }
Another body of work incorporates a VLM in some capacity to improve the performance of a downstream VLA \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme,shi2025hirobot}.

\textbf{Accounting for robot capabilities. }
A complementary line of work conditions the \emph{language} side of a
system on what the robot can actually do: SayCan grounds an LLM planner in
learned value functions so that proposed plans respect the robot's
skills~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, and Inner Monologue closes the loop with
execution feedback~\cite{huang2022inner}. Our rephraser is the
instruction-level analogue: rather than selecting \emph{which} skill to
invoke, it learns which \emph{surface forms} of an instruction this
executor parses reliably, and edits the instruction accordingly.

\section{Setup}

Throughout, we work with two modern VLAs: $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$. All of our $\pi_0$ experiments are conducted on the Bridge dataset, and all of our $\pi_{0.5}$ experiments are conducted on the LIBERO dataset. The specific versions of our models are finetuned for their respective datasets and are inherited from prior works. Moreover, the finetuned version of $\pi_0$ that we use is explicitly trained on rephrases of each task in order to reduce its language-sensitivity. That is, in the case of $\pi_0$, we are already working with a model that's been developed to some extent to mitigate the issue of language-sensitivity, and in this work we seek to further explore what remains of its language sensitivity. In our own tests, the rephrase augmentation is worth a 20\% relative improvement on natural rephrasings (28.4\% vs 23.8\% pooled success on a 192-phrase natural-rephrase set, against the otherwise-identical non-augmented checkpoint); we seek to further improve robustness with rules.

In our experiments, we measure the success of these VLAs in simulation. In determining success, there's a design choice to be made: at which frame in the episode should success be measured? For Bridge's simulation (SIMPLER) tasks, we measure success at the 60th frame of a trajectory, defaulting to the benchmark's default.

\subsection{Tasks}

Each dataset used in our experiments is associated with a set of simulatable tasks. Each task is associated with a canonical instruction phrase provided by its benchmark. Next, we describe the tasks.

\subsubsection{Bridge / SIMPLER}

SIMPLER tasks are simulatable tabletop tasks, such as ``put carrot on plate'' and ``put eggplant into yellow basket''. In our experiments, we use four tasks which are shipped with the benchmark, and we further construct 16 more tasks using benchmark-provided primitives. In total, this gives us 20 simulatable tasks. Of these, eight are in vocabulary in that they share a common object with tasks in the Bridge training set. Task success is procedurally determined by the benchmark.

\subsubsection{LIBERO}

LIBERO consists of many simulatable tabletop tasks. Examples include ``put the bowl on the plate'' and ``put the moka pot on the stove''. Its tasks are split into 5 groups: libero\_goal, libero\_spatial, libero\_object, libero\_90, and libero\_10. We inherit a $\pi_{0.5}$ that is finetuned on all of these categories with the exception of libero\_90. As in SIMPLER, task success is defined procedurally by the benchmark's code. Each LIBERO task has a maximum number of steps for which it is run, and the task is determined to be successful once any step is determined to be successful.

\subsection{Methodology}

We do our best to exclude pairs where a minimal edit introduces ambiguity with respect to the scene. Our search algorithm is simple: first we generate many rephrases for each task (in fact, we use the exact evidence files used by our rules' distillers). Then, with the help of an LLM, we propose sets to test, based on the evidence, and we evaluate each phrase with many attempts in simulation. After gathering success rates per phrase, we retain only those sets where the difference in accuracy between the best and worst phrase is statistically significant, that is, they are unlikely to be explained by chance (see the lists for their exact p-scores). We manually filter out phrase sets with large gaps that are potentially explained by scene-based ambiguity. For $\pi_0$, we evaluate each phrase 72 times (24 layouts by 3 repetitions). For $\pi_{0.5}$, we evaluate each phrase once over 50 layouts. To further reduce noise in our measurements, we use common random seeds per layout / repetition pair. We note that it was qualitatively more challenging to find these phrase sets for $\pi_{0.5}$ than it was to find them for $\pi_0$.

\subsection{Oracle Search}

To further quantify language sensitivity, for each task, we compare its ``best-performing'' phrase to its canonical phrase (see Setup for the definition of canonical phrase). To find the ``best'' phrase for a task, we employ a simple algorithm. For a fixed task, we first generate 16 rephrases using a VLM. We then score each phrase by rolling it out multiple times. Then, we ask the VLM to generate 16 new phrases (giving another set of 16) based on all previously scored phrases. We repeat this process three times, and select the best-performing phrase. See Figures XX and YY for a comparison of the success rates of oracle phrases to canonicals, adversarials, and naturals. For both models, oracle phrases reach significantly higher success rates than the other phrase sets. However, according to this metric, $\pi_0$ is more sensitive to phrasing than $\pi_{0.5}$, consistent with our experience when searching for minimally-edited phrase sets with large gaps. [See the appendix for the full list of oracle phrases across searched tasks.]

\section{Reducing VLA language sensitivity by LLM-based learning of rephrasing rules}

Given that VLAs can be sensitive to task phrasing, we here present our method for reducing their effective phrase sensitivity. An overview of our method is given in Fig XX. At a high level, it is quite simple: generate many rephrases across many tasks, score each phrase, present these scored phrases to an LLM, and then receive a set of rules specifying how to rephrase an incoming language instruction. It is worth noting that we do not aim to optimize task-phrasing for a specific, fixed task. Rather, we aim to optimize task-phrasing across all tasks at once. Now, we explain the method in more detail.

\subsection{Gathering Evidence: Generating and Scoring Phrases}

In order to derive phrasing rules, we first gather a large set of phrases. For each task in our training set, we prompt a VLM to generate 16 ``natural'' phrases --- e.g. the way a human might naturally request a task --- and 8 adversarial phrases --- e.g. a convoluted, overly complicated task request. For example, a natural rephrase of ``put the carrot on the plate'' might be ``...'', whereas an adversarial one might be ``...''. We also include all phrases measured during oracle search (these tend to be more benign rephrases). See the appendix for the prompt used to generate natural and adversarial phrases.

\subsubsection{Scoring Phrases}

Rule-distillation requires that each phrase gets a score, so that phrases are comparable. For simulatable tasks, we simply simulate each task / phrase pair multiple times to get a success rate. However, we also aim to measure phrases for non-simulatable tasks: these tasks are equipped with real-world expert trajectories. For these, we score rephrases using a simple metric that acts as a proxy for success rate without actually measuring rollout success. For each phrase, we calculate the gripper error between the ground truth action and the VLA's predicted action over 16 trajectories at 4 frames per trajectory. This gives us a set of 64 measurements which we average and use as the score. When using this proxy to measure task / phrase pairs that are simulatable, we see that their scores correlate relatively well with actual rollout success.

\subsection{Distilling Rules}

To distill the evidence into a set of rules, we prompt Claude to derive rules as in the appendix. We test three different rulebooks which differ by the evidence they are provided: in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both. Since rule-distillation is stochastic, we distill each type of rulebook three times, and during evaluation we average their results. In our experiments, rulebooks contain somewhere between 10 and 20 rules.

For rulebooks distilled from phrases from the finetuning dataset, we further provide the full set of finetuning phrases so that the distiller may make use of corpus statistics such as how frequently objects occur in the finetuning data, the most common phrase structures, etc. Due to cost constraints, we use Claude Fable to generate the first three rulebooks (one each of in-finetune, out-of-finetune, and both), and then we repeat twice more with Claude Opus 5. Anyway, our results don't suggest that this has a significant effect when we evaluate the distilled rules.

All distilled rulebooks are available in the project
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/sttawm/phrase-rl/tree/main/paper/rules}}

\subsubsection{Applying Rules}

Rephrasing rules are applied just once before the robot's trajectory begins. In addition to the task instruction and the rules, the applier is also presented with a reasoning trace, itself a function of the task / phrase and an image of the scene. These traces are generated by gemini-3.5-flash. The rules are applied greedily, and the output phrase is given directly to the VLA, after which the robot performs the task as usual.



\section{Rules' Results \& Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:rules-results}

We evaluate $\pi_0$, finetuned on Bridge with rephrase augmentation, in simulation with SIMPLER tasks. Note that this model is finetuned on Bridge such that each task gets rephrased 16 times, so that the model is already trained to be less sensitive to instruction phrasing.

\subsection{Phrase Sets}

We evaluate on four different phrase sets: adversarials, VLM-generated naturals, human-generated naturals, and canonicals. We briefly define these categories. Adversarial and VLM-Generated Natural phrases are generated by gemini-3.5-flash, given the scene image, using prompt XX given in the appendix. Adversarial phrases retain the goal, but are awkwardly worded. VLM-generated naturals are supposed to mimic the way a human might request a task. Human-generated naturals are gathered from friends and family via survey: the user is presented with a successful video demonstration of each task and asked to request the task of an 8-year-old, an adult, and a robot. We evaluate 6 adversarial phrases per task (72 in total), roughly 16 phrases per task for the VLM-generated naturals (186 in total), and roughly 33 phrases per task for the human-generated naturals (392 in total; 363 unique task / phrase pairs). We additionally evaluate the SIMPLER environment's canonical instruction accompanying each task, but we note that at just one phrase per task, one should be cautious in assigning significance to the canonical category's results (given in the appendix).

\subsection{Baselines}

We evaluate against three baselines: no rephraser, no-rules rephraser, and CoVer. The no-rephraser does not rephrase at all and passes the incoming instruction directly to the VLA. The no-rules rephraser contains a single simple rule (see appendix). CoVer uses a contrastively trained verifier to continuously select its verifier's highest-scoring phrase / action pair from a set of 40 (8 rephrases by 5 actions each). Each task is evaluated on 24 different layouts of the scene.

\subsection{Statistical tests}
\label{sec:stats}

For a comparison between two arms (e.g.\ a rulebook cell against the
un-rephrased baseline), the paired unit is the \emph{base instruction}. For
base $i$, let $c_i$ be the arm's success rate on that base --- the success
proportion of the arm's rephrasing of base $i$ over the base's pinned
layout grid, averaged over the three independent rulebook draws where the
arm is a rulebook cell --- and let $b_i$ be the baseline's success
proportion for the raw base over the same grid. The paired difference is
$d_i = c_i - b_i$, and the reported effect is the mean of the $d_i$
(base-weighted, matching all pooled numbers in this paper). Because both
arms face identical pinned layouts and common random numbers, scene
difficulty cancels within each $d_i$.

The p-value is a two-sided sign-flip permutation test on the mean of the
$d_i$: under the null hypothesis that the arm changes nothing, each
$d_i$'s sign is exchangeable, so we recompute the mean under random sign
assignments (20{,}000 resamples; exact enumeration of all $2^n$ sign
patterns when $n \le 12$) and report the fraction of null means at least
as large in magnitude as the observed one. The number of bases is the
test's sample size ($n = 186$ VLM-generated natural, $363$
human-generated natural, $72$ adversarial, $12$ canonical);
layouts and repetitions sharpen each $d_i$ but do not add to $n$.
Averaging draws before pairing means the test reads on the average-draw
rulebook: it licenses claims about the distilled books, not about the
distillation procedure (with three draws, a draw-level test is reported
only as a replication check). Baseline per-base rates come from the published baseline rolls: the
un-rephrased natural set at one repetition; for the adversarial condition,
the 60 generated attacks at one repetition plus the original 12 attacks at
twelve; for the human-generated naturals, the raw survey phrases at one
repetition; for the canonical condition, the 12 canonical instructions at
twelve repetitions. Cell arms carry one or two repetitions depending on
condition and draw. Repetition counts affect only the precision of the
individual $d_i$, not the pairing.

\subsection{Results}

Our best-performing rulebook type is consistently the out-of-finetune rulebook (the exception to this rule is the canonical set, where the in-finetune rulebook performs best; however, we note that at just a single phrase per task, our evaluation of canonical phrases is statistically underpowered). The rulebook performs significantly better on out-of-distribution tasks. With the out-of-finetune rulebooks, the Claude applier increases performance, on average, by more than 20\% across adversarial and natural phrases when compared to the no-rephraser baseline. When compared to the no-rules rephraser baseline, it improves performance by 26\% on adversarials, 11\% on human-generated naturals, and 4.7\% on VLM-generated naturals. See figures XX and YY for the detailed set of results.
