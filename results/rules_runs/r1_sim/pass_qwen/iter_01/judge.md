===RULE NOTES===
1. **Short plain imperative, same objects and goal.** Adherence is high — every rewrite is an imperative in roughly the right shape, and no rewrite loses the object or the goal. Performance: the book improved on its predecessor, +0.087 (0.316 vs 0.229 unrephrased) against +0.076 last time, and the regime where this rule does its work — full rewrites of ornate/polite bases — averages +14.8 rollout points per pair (n=18), with every measurement now same-draw so those deltas are finally trustworthy. One calibration for everything below: three of the seven verbatim copies show −16.7 despite identical strings, which is exactly one episode out of six; treat ±16.7 on any single row as noise and trust only repeated patterns. Keep rule 1 unchanged.

2. **Output format.** Perfect adherence for the third straight sample — forty rewrites, zero labels, quotes, or commentary. Costless insurance; keep verbatim.

3. **Copy guard with two licensed edits.** Adherence improved only if you count the licence: 6 of 22 copy-eligible bases were copied verbatim, ~6 more received insertion-only colour edits (licensed), leaving ~10 unlicensed edits — call it 55% compliant, up from ~45%, but the specific clauses written to stop repeat offences failed again: "set stays set" was violated twice ('set the carrot on the wheel' → 'place…', 'Move the orange vegetable' → 'Place…') with "set" literally named in the rule text, and one copy came back with changed capitalisation and an added period. Performance: the copies themselves are delta-noise (−8.3 mean over 6, entirely the one-episode artifact — a copy cannot causally move the score), while the unlicensed edits netted approximately zero with huge variance: verb swaps +33.3/0/0, but trades −50 and −16.7 twice. Two findings matter for the next book. First, the verb-family clause is both the least obeyed part of the rule and the least supported by evidence — put/place/set/lay swaps measure within noise everywhere in the bank — so it is spending enforcement credibility on a distinction that doesn't pay; cut the verb examples from the byte-exactness list and let the guard concentrate on what does pay: nouns, modifiers, colours, spatial words. Second, one unlicensed edit class has now won twice at +33.3: adding the missing verb to a verbless fragment ('green block on yellow block' → 'place the green block on the yellow block'; same effect last iteration). That deserves to become licence (c): "if the instruction has no verb, prefix one (put/place) and change nothing else" — pending the re-measure suggested below. If adherence is still ~50% after a third rewording, stop rewording; the copy decision belongs in the applier's scaffold as a mechanical pre-check, not in prose.

4. **Verb-first, delete politeness and framing.** Adherence perfect again ('Please shift' → 'Shift…' +16.7; every "assuming/hereby/should you be capable" clause gone without residue). Performance: it is the precondition for the +14.8 full-rewrite regime. Keep verbatim — this is the most settled rule in the book.

5. **Keep the frame; translate roundabout actions into one clause; no pick-up-and-put expansion.** Adherence is split down the middle. The move/take/transfer avoidance was followed — no full rewrite opens with them (it was even over-applied to a copy-eligible base, 'Transfer the carrot over to the wheel.' → 'Place the carrot on the wheel.', +33.3, technically a rule 3 violation that won). But the no-expansion clause was broken about seven times: one-goal ornate bases ('You are hereby instructed to commence the relocation of the carrot…') still came back as 'Pick up the carrot and put it on the plate.' Performance vindicates the clause emphatically: within this sample, single-clause full rewrites average ≈45 (33.3, 16.7, 33.3, 33.3, 66.7, 100, 33.3) while pick-up-and-put rewrites average ≈14 (0, 0, 0, 16.7, 0, 33.3, 50), and the head-to-head on carrot_on_plate is 33.3 vs 16.7/0 for the same base pool. This is a good rule nobody fully executes — fix adherence, not content. Give it a template the applier can match: "a one-goal rewrite has the shape VERB + the OBJECT + PREPOSITION + the GOAL, and contains no 'and'"; keep the two-step exception only for bases that genuinely narrate a grasp phase ('Proceed to grasp… and subsequently…'), where the expansion is arguably faithful.

6. **No invention, no trades, colour-insertion licence.** The licence half is the iteration's success: insertion-only colour edits on copy-eligible bases netted +13.9 mean (n=6: +66.7 'red cola can', +16.7 'purple eggplant', +16.7 'red coke can', one −16.7, two 0s), and colour-enriched full rewrites took the sample's best deltas (+50 'green spoon… blue towel'). Adherence to the licence's own conditions is good but not clean: the applier inserted "green plate" twice and "light green plate" once on the coke task, where the bank's only colour evidence for that plate ('yellow plate', −16 to −19) suggests colour-guessing, and "light green" is a compound the licence never contemplated — both coke 'green plate' rewrites rolled 0. The ban half repeated last iteration's failure exactly: 'aluminum' → 'soda' happened again in the worst row of the sample (−50) with both words named in the rule, plus cube→block (−16.7) and one→block resolution (−16.7). Two iterations of increasingly explicit example-naming have not moved this behaviour, and the banned trades keep losing while the licensed insertions keep winning — so the content is right and the delivery is exhausted. Restate the ban as a checkable output invariant instead of examples: "every noun and adjective of the base must appear unchanged in the rewrite, unless rule 4 deleted its whole clause or rule 7 translated it"; the applier can verify that token by token. Tighten the licence with a closed colour list (black, white, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple — no compounds like "light green") and keep "when unsure add nothing". The coke-plate probe below settles whether those green-plate insertions were scene-true.

7. **Translate descriptions and unusual nouns to everyday words.** Adherence on descriptions remains excellent and keeps producing the top of the table ('concave piece of silverware' → 'spoon', 'device used for typing' → 'keyboard', 'elongated orange taproot' → 'orange carrot' +33.3, 'cylindrical cola container' → 'red can'). But the newly added unusual-noun clause was ignored in the one place it could fire: '…securely housed in the ramekin' → 'put it in the ramekin.' (rolled 0), with ramekin→bowl being the rule's own printed example and the bank showing bowl-phrasings 30–40 points above ramekin-phrasings at n=64. The likely culprit is the rule's own fence — "an ordinary stated noun is not unusual — leave it alone" arrives last and plausibly reclassified "ramekin" as an ordinary stated noun. Fix the collision, not the idea: state the test concretely ("if it is not a word people use weekly in a kitchen — ramekin, vessel, receptacle, basin — translate it; pot, pan, bowl, cup, plate, wheel, keyboard stay") and put the fence before the examples so the examples are read as passing the test, not fighting it.

8. **Keep spatial detail; keep "on top of" whole; six to nine words.** Adherence is good on the new clauses: 'put the carrot on top of the plate' survived intact this time (it was compressed last iteration), and both 'atop' bases were translated to 'on top of' as instructed. One clear violation: 'place the red can upright inside the white bowl' → 'place the red can in the white bowl' dropped "upright" (and inside→in), delta 0 — orientation words are stated spatial detail but the rule's example list doesn't name them, and the applier evidently reads the list as exhaustive. Add "upright / standing up" to the parenthetical list; nothing else about the rule needs to move. The length target held (most rewrites 6–9 words) except exactly where rule 5's expansions pushed to 10–12 — the two rules fail together, so rule 5's template fix serves this rule too. The source-clause and or-choice protections remain completely unexercised for the second sample in a row; they cost nothing, but they are unfalsifiable passengers until the bank contains bases with sources and or-choices, which the suggestions below request.

===SUGGESTIONS===
Measure 'put the coke can on the green plate' vs 'put the coke can on the plate' — if green drops like yellow did (−16 to −19), the applier's coke-task colour insertions were scene-false guesses and the licence needs harder gating; if it holds, the −50 row's damage narrows to the aluminum→soda trade.
Measure 'put the red aluminum can on the plate' vs 'put the red coke can on the plate' — tests whether keeping "aluminum" (the ban's flagship example) is actually policy-friendly or merely neutral.
Measure 'Grab that red aluminum can and place it on the plate.' vs its base 'Grab that red aluminum can and set it on the plate.' — isolates the verb swap from the noun trade in the sample's worst row.
Re-measure the pair 'green block on yellow block' vs 'place the green block on the yellow block' at n≥60 — verb-prefixing a verbless fragment has won +33.3 twice at n=6; if it replicates, rule 3 gains licence (c).
Re-measure 'Put the green one on top of the yellow one.' vs 'Put the green block on top of the yellow block.' at n≥60 — the vague-noun-preservation clause rests on two conflicting thin draws (−16.7 here, opposite sign in the older bank).
Measure 'Pick up the carrot and put it on the plate.' vs 'Put the carrot on the plate.' same-draw at n≥60 — the 45-vs-14 single-versus-expansion pattern is the book's biggest behavioural claim and currently rests on n=6 rows.
Measure 'pick up the red can and put it in the white bowl' vs 'pick up the red can and put it in the ramekin' — the rule 7 unusual-noun licence in exactly the frame the applier produced, closing the ramekin file.
Measure 'take the carrot off the table and put it on the plate' vs 'put the carrot on the plate' — first source-stated two-step base in this bank; tells us whether rule 8's source protection and rule 5's two-step exception ever earn their text on these tasks.
