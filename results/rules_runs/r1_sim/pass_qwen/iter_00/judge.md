===RULE NOTES===
1. **Short plain imperative, same objects and goal.** Adherence is high: all 40 rewrites are one-clause or two-clause imperatives, averaging seven to eight words, and none drops the target object or goal location. Performance: this rule is essentially the book's thesis, and the book's measured delta is positive — 0.306 with rules vs 0.229 unrephrased, +0.076 — with the clearest wins coming exactly where this rule bites hardest: the ornate legalese inputs (base proxy logits of −2 to −6.4) turn into short imperatives scoring 16.7–100 on the rewrite scale. One caution on reading the numbers that applies to every rule below: the per-sample deltas in the file are not trustworthy. Rewrite logits are quantized to sixths (0, 16.7, 33.3, …, 100) while base logits are continuous and marked `base_logit_same_draw: false`, and the nine verbatim copies — where the policy's behavior cannot have changed — show "deltas" of +0.9 to +49.5. That spread is the noise/scale floor. I therefore judge performance from the book-level aggregate and from within-task comparisons of rewrite logits only. On that reading, rule 1 is doing its job; keep it unchanged.

2. **Output format only.** Perfect adherence: every output is a single line of plain text, no labels, no quotes, no commentary, across 40 samples including the heavily rewritten ones. Performance is not separable from the book, but there are zero format failures to pay for, and one leaked "Thinking Process:" would score catastrophically. This is cheap insurance; keep it verbatim.

3. **Copy short clean instructions verbatim.** This is the least-obeyed rule in the book. Of roughly twenty copy-eligible inputs (≤12 words, no politeness, no roundabout description), nine were copied and about eleven were edited anyway: "set the carrot on the keyboard/wheel" → "put…", "put the coke down on the plate" lost "down", "place the carrot across the black keyboard" lost "black", "Transfer the carrot over to the wheel" → "Place…", "Slide the eggplant into the yellow basket" gained "purple", "Stack the green on the yellow" gained "block" twice, "place the cola can inside the white bowl" gained "red", telegraphic "green block on yellow block" gained "put". Adherence ≈ 45%, and the rule's own strongest language ("character for character", keep small words and stated colours) is what got broken. Performance is the interesting part: the violations were not punished, and the detail-adding ones were among the best scores in the sample (see rule 6), while several faithful copies sat at the bottom within their tasks — "put the coke can on the dish" copied → 0 while "put the coke on the plate" → 50 on the same task; "Move the orange vegetable to the wheel" copied → 0 while the description-resolved "Place the carrot on the wheel" → 33.3. So we have low adherence and no visible cost to the disobedience — which undermines the rule's premise that copying is safest. But the whole-book measurement cannot separate "copying is fine" from "the applier's edits are fine", because the copy cases are by construction the already-clean inputs. What to do: keep a copy default but make the trigger mechanical and narrower — copy verbatim only when the instruction already uses the canonical vocabulary (put/place/pick up/stack + on/in/into, plain object nouns); when it is short but uses an off-vocabulary word ("set", "dish", "transfer"), allow a one-word canonicalization rather than forbidding all edits. Before committing to that, this is the rule I would most want isolated: score the nine copied strings and their lightly-canonicalized variants ("set"→"put", "dish"→"plate") in the same draw. If canonicalization moves nothing, restore the strict copy rule with harder wording; if it moves scores the way "dish"→0 suggests, the character-for-character clause should go. The truncated-direction carve-out ("from top down" → "from top to bottom") never fired in this sample and is untested either way.

4. **Start with the verb; delete politeness and framing.** High adherence: every output begins with an action verb; "please", "Could you… for me", "It would be highly appreciated…", "You are hereby instructed…", "Assuming/Provided that…", "Should you be capable…" are all gone, with no residue. Performance within-task looks good: "Can you place the green block onto the yellow block for me?" (base −2.5) became "Stack the green block on the yellow block." at 66.7, the best stack phrasing; "Please shift the carrot onto the plate." → "Shift…" at 33.3, above that task's median. Nothing in the sample argues against it and the ornate inputs it targets have the worst base logits in the file. Keep unchanged.

5. **Keep the verb-and-preposition frame; translate only roundabout actions.** Moderate adherence. The applier kept frames often but swapped verbs freely on copy-eligible inputs ("set"→"put" twice, "Transfer over to"→"Place on"), replaced verbs wholesale during full rewrites ("Can you place…"→"Stack…"), once kept a roundabout verb it was told to translate ("swiftly conveyed"→"Convey the carrot to the plate", which scored 50, oddly the best carrot-on-plate result), and repeatedly expanded single-goal ornate inputs into a two-clause "Pick up X and put/place it on Y" that no rule asked for. Performance gives a real within-task signal on that last habit: the two-clause form tends to lose to the single-verb form on the same task — carrot_on_plate: "Pick up the carrot and put it on the plate" 16.7/16.7 vs "Shift/place…" 33.3/33.3; ramekin: "Pick up the red can and put it in the white ramekin" 16.7 vs single-verb variants 33.3–50; stack: "Pick up the green block and put/place it…" 50/33.3 vs "Stack…" 66.7 three times. Meanwhile the frame-keeping mandate itself shows no payoff: the frame-breaking "Stack" rewrites are the task's best. What to do: relax "keep the frame exactly" to "keep the preposition and goal structure", and add the thing the data actually supports — during a full rewrite, prefer the single-verb form ("put/place/stack X on/in Y") over "pick up X and put it on Y" unless the base genuinely names two separate steps. The "translate roundabout verbs to plain ones" half stays; "Convey" at 50 is one noisy sample, not a counterexample worth acting on.

6. **Never add detail; never resolve vague nouns.** Low adherence, and this is the book's central finding: the violations of this rule are the top of the sample. "Slide the eggplant…" gained "purple" → 100; "the cloth"/"that spoon" became "green-handled spoon"/"blue cloth" → 50 and 50, against 16.7 and 16.7 for the color-free spoon rewrites on the same task; "cola can" became "red can"/"red soda can" → 50 and 50, against 33.3 for the variant that added nothing; "the green"/"the green one" became "green block" → 66.7, 66.7, 18.6, against 33.3 for the faithful "green cube on the yellow one" copy. Conversely, the rule's success cases underperform: "the orange vegetable" preserved verbatim → 0, while the same task's description-resolved "carrot" → 33.3. The applier clearly has scene knowledge (it knew the eggplant is purple and the spoon green-handled) and used it despite the ban, and the policy — evidently trained on color-qualified canonical phrases like "green block" and "blue cloth" — rewarded it. The rule's core is still right in one respect: the lone hallucination-shaped trade, "red aluminum can" → "red soda can", produced the coke-on-plate task's worst score (0), and that is the failure mode the rule exists to prevent. What to do: invert the scope rather than drop it. Replace the blanket ban with: never *invent* a detail you cannot verify and never *contradict* a stated one; but when the scene or task context confirms an object's colour or canonical name, prefer the grounded canonical phrase ("purple eggplant", "green block", "blue cloth"), and resolve vague nouns ("the green one", "the orange vegetable") to the confirmed object name. Because this is the largest claim in the audit and rides on quantized within-task scores, it needs a controlled experiment before the next book commits: same instructions, enriched vs unenriched, scored in the same draw (the eggplant task won't discriminate — both variants hit 100, a ceiling).

7. **Replace descriptions with everyday kitchen words.** High adherence and it reads well: "concave piece of silverware" → "spoon", "device used for typing" → "keyboard", "elongated orange taproot" → "carrot", "cylindrical cola container" → "red can", "woven textile" → "cloth", with no "vessel/receptacle/implement" leakage anywhere. Performance within-task supports it: the taproot input had the sample's worst base (−6.4) and its rewrite scored 33.3; the silverware monstrosity (base 1.1 but a 21-word sentence) → 50. One instructive wrinkle: the applier once kept "ramekin" ("…put it in the white ramekin", 16.7) while every "white bowl" phrasing on the same task scored 33.3–50 — the ordinary-noun preference this rule encodes is right, but rule 6 forbids trading a *stated* "ramekin" for "bowl", so the two rules pull against each other exactly where the policy's vocabulary matters. Same tension on "the orange vegetable": rule 7 wants it resolved as a description, rule 6 protects "vegetable" as a real noun, and the applier's rule-6-compliant copy scored 0. Keep rule 7 as written, and resolve the boundary in rule 6's rewrite: adjective-plus-generic-noun phrases ("orange vegetable", "the green one") should be treated as descriptions for rule 7 to resolve, not nouns for rule 6 to preserve.

8. **Keep spatial detail, ~10 words, cut filler only.** Adherence is good on what the sample exercises: "upright", "across", "in/on/inside" survive every rewrite, and every output is ≤12 words. The drops that did happen ("down", "on top of", "black" from "black keyboard") occurred on copy-eligible inputs, so they are really rule 3 failures, and none shows a within-task penalty ("across the keyboard" without "black" scored 16.7, the best keyboard result — though that task floors at 0–16.7 for everything, so it proves little). The clauses about keeping the source ("out of the pot", "from the basket") and both halves of an "or" choice never fired — the sample contains no instruction with a stated source, an "or", or a "from A to B" span, so those clauses are entirely unmeasured. Keep the rule, but know that half of it is untested; the sampling for the next iteration should include source-bearing and either/or instructions, or those clauses are dead weight we can't evaluate.

===SUGGESTIONS===
Score the nine verbatim-copied strings again in the same draw as their bases — identical text currently shows "deltas" up to +49.5, and this calibrates the noise floor every other comparison sits on.
"put the spoon on the blue cloth" vs "put the green-handled spoon on the blue cloth" vs "pick up the green-handled spoon and put it on the blue cloth" — separates scene-grounded enrichment (rule 6's inversion) from the two-clause expansion (rule 5's finding) on one task.
"put the green block on the yellow block" vs "stack the green block on the yellow block" vs "stack the green cube on the yellow cube" — tests canonical task-verb substitution and block-vs-cube vocabulary; 66.7-vs-33.3 here predicts "stack"+"block" wins.
"put the coke can on the plate" vs "put the coke can on the dish" vs "put the coke on the plate" — tests synonym canonicalization toward training vocabulary; the copied "dish" scoring 0 predicts "plate" wins.
"put the red can in the white bowl" vs "put the red can in the white ramekin" — same question for bowl/ramekin, deciding whether rule 7's ordinary-noun preference should override a stated odd noun.
"set the carrot on the plate" vs "put the carrot on the plate" vs "place the carrot on the plate" — isolates the set/put/place verb swap the applier keeps making, on a task with headroom (keyboard, where it was observed, floors at 0).
"move the orange vegetable to the wheel" vs "move the carrot to the wheel" vs "place the carrot on the wheel" — tests whether resolving a vague noun to the confirmed object name helps, the rule 6/7 boundary case.
"pick up the carrot and put it on the plate" vs "put the carrot on the plate" — the two-clause-vs-single-verb question in its purest form, same draw.
"put the eggplant in the basket" vs "put the purple eggplant in the yellow basket" — the eggplant task hit 100 both ways; a harder variant (drop the basket colour) is needed to see whether enrichment matters or the task is just at ceiling.
Add to the sample pool instructions with a stated source ("take the carrot out of the pot"), an either/or goal ("put the spoon on the plate or the towel"), and a truncated span ("fold it from top down") — rule 3's completion carve-out and rule 8's source/or clauses currently have zero coverage.
