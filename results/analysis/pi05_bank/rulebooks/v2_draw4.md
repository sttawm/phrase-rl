===RULES===
1. First, write the instruction as a bare lowercase imperative with no trailing punctuation, starting with the verb: strip request, desire and filler wrappers ("can you", "could you", "would you", "please", "just", "go ahead and", and the exact phrase "right up to" -> "to"); turn declarative or desire frames into the plain command ("i want X on Y" / "X goes on Y" / "X should be put on Y" -> "put X on Y"; "the stove should be turned on" / "i want the stove on" -> "turn on the stove"); write "the" instead of "that" or "this". Keep every word after the verb otherwise the same; the later rules see this unwrapped form.
2. Keep every specific or unusual object name the incoming instruction already uses exactly as it is (ramekin, moka pot, rack, caddy, tray, basket, wine bottle, cream cheese box) and never replace it with a commoner or vaguer word. When the incoming instruction uses a looser head noun for an object or fixture and the scene description names exactly one object that word can denote, write the scene's head noun instead (with a scene that lists a rack, "stand", "wooden stand" and "holder" become "rack"; with a moka pot, "coffee pot" and "espresso pot" become "moka pot"; with a wine bottle, "wine jug" becomes "wine bottle"). The scene description is the authority: if it names the object differently, use its name; if it names no such object, or two or more objects in the scene fit the word, keep the incoming word. This rule changes head nouns only: it never adds or changes an adjective (rule 8), it never touches the interchangeable pairs of rule 12 (cabinet/cupboard, mug/cup, compartment/slot, bottom/lower, top/upper), which stay as given, and it does not apply to the word "dish" (rule 3), to cooking-appliance words (rule 4) or to product category words such as bottle, can or sauce (rule 7), which those rules decide.
3. Handle the word "dish" by its role. (a) When "dish" is the destination of a placement or the landmark that locates the target ("on the dish", "next to the dish", "to the dish") and the instruction does not name "the plate" anywhere else: if the scene lists a plate, write "plate"; if the scene lists no plate, keep "dish". (b) When "dish" is the landmark locating the target and the instruction already names "the plate" elsewhere ("the bowl next to the dish ... on the plate"): if the scene lists exactly one object other than the plate and the target that "dish" could denote (for example a ramekin), write that object's scene name; otherwise keep "dish". (c) When "dish" is the destination and the instruction already names "the plate" elsewhere ("the bowl next to the plate ... on the dish"), keep "dish". (d) When "dish" is the object being slid or pushed along a surface and the scene lists a plate, write "plate". (e) When "dish" is the object being lifted and put somewhere ("put the dish on the plate", "put the black dish in the drawer", "put the dish on top of the cabinet"), keep it as given. Whenever "dish" is replaced, drop an adjective that came with it (flat, round) unless it is part of the scene's name for the replacement.
4. When the scene description lists one cooking appliance and no separate hot plate or griddle, call that appliance "the stove" wherever the instruction mentions it, replacing the whole appliance noun phrase including its adjectives: hob, cooktop, stovetop, range, cooker, griddle, heating element, burner, burners, stove burner, hotplate, hot plate, electric stove and electric hot plate all become "the stove", whether it is being switched on or something is being put on it. If the scene lists a separate hot plate or griddle in addition to the stove, keep the incoming word.
5. For a command that asks for the stove to be on, write exactly "switch on the stove" (rule 1 has already unwrapped the frame and rule 4 has already made the noun "stove"): rewrite "turn on the stove", "power on the stove", "fire up the stove", "ignite the stove", "start the stove", "activate the stove", "light the stove", and mechanism descriptions such as "turn the black knob to start the burner", to "switch on the stove". Keep any following clause unchanged ("switch on the stove and put the moka pot on it"). Do not apply this to switching off, and do not write "switch on" or "start" with any appliance noun other than "stove".
6. For switching the stove off write "turn off the stove" (rewrite switch off, shut off, power off, kill). When the direct object is a drawer, door, cabinet or microwave, write "close" (rewrite shut, push in, push closed, slide shut) and "open" (rewrite pull open, pull out, slide open); do not apply these when the direct object is anything else ("pull out the book from the caddy" is not "open the book"). Name the outcome, never the mechanism: no knob, handle, button or switch.
7. Name a product by the specific name the scene description gives it (ketchup, bbq sauce, tomato sauce, salad dressing, orange juice, milk, alphabet soup, chocolate pudding, butter, cream cheese box). A word that names only the packaging or class of a product (bottle, can, tin, carton, jar, box, cup, drink, food, snack, sauce, condiment, container, item, thing) is a category word, except when the object really is a cup or mug or the scene's own name contains that word (a mug called "cup" and a "cream cheese box" are not category words and stay as given). If the incoming instruction uses a category word, with or without an adjective ("the red bottle", "the can"), and exactly one product in the scene fits it, judging fit by that product's usual packaging and colour, replace the whole phrase, adjective included, with that product's scene name; if two or more products could fit, or the scene does not let you tell, keep the incoming words unchanged rather than guess. Never rewrite a specific name into a category word, never shorten a specific name, and never swap the head word of a name for a category word; a shortened name that keeps its head word and fits exactly one product in the scene (soup, dressing, juice) needs no expansion.
8. Do not add any adjective the incoming instruction lacks (no colour, material, shape, size, finish or position word: not "black" to a bowl the instruction calls "the bowl", not "wooden" to a cabinet, not "round" to a plate), and keep every adjective the instruction gives, in particular colour and position words (black, white, red, yellow and white, middle, back, left, right, top, bottom), which pick out one of several similar objects. Drop an adjective only when the noun it was attached to is replaced by rule 2, 3, 4 or 7 and the adjective described the replaced word rather than the scene's object ("wooden stand" -> "rack", "flat dish" -> "plate", "electric hot plate" -> "the stove", "red bottle" -> "ketchup"); an adjective on a correctly named noun stays ("silver moka pot", "wooden cabinet", "round plate"). One exception in the colour direction: if the instruction's colour word is a shade or near-synonym of the colour in the scene's name for the object ("grey" or "dark" for a bowl the scene calls "black bowl") and the scene lists only one object of that type, write the scene's colour word; if the scene lists several objects of that type, keep the incoming colour word.
9. In the destination phrase of a put, place, set or "pick up X and place it" command, use the plain trained preposition: "on top of" when the destination is the top surface of a cabinet, cupboard, shelf or other piece of furniture (rewrite atop, up onto, up on, onto, on to the top of, and a bare "on the cabinet" used as a destination); "on" when the destination is a plate, stove, rack, another bowl or any other object that is not furniture (rewrite onto, on to, over onto, and "on top of the plate" -> "on the plate"); "in" when the destination is a drawer, basket, tray, compartment, slot, microwave or caddy (rewrite into, inside, inside of). Leave every preposition that describes where an object currently is untouched ("the bowl on the ramekin", "the bowl in the top drawer"), and leave relational placements (in front of, to the front of, to the right of, on the right of, next to, beside) as given.
10. When the verb is slide, scoot or shove and the destination is a place on the same surface ("to the front of", "to the edge of", "toward", "next to", "closer to"), write "push the <object> to <place>" ("slide that plate over to the front of the stove" -> "push the plate to the front of the stove"): drop "over", "along" and "across", write "the" for "that", and keep the object and destination words. Do not apply this when the destination is "in", "into" or "onto" something (a slot, basket, drawer, plate), and never turn put, place, move, bring or carry into "push"; those describe lifting and setting down.
11. Apart from the rewrites in rules 5, 6 and 10, leave the manipulation verb and the clause structure as given: put, place and set are interchangeable, as are pick up, grab and lift; "pick up X and place it in Y" and "put X in Y" are equally good; keep two-part instructions as two parts ("put the black bowl in the bottom drawer of the cabinet and close it", "switch on the stove and put the moka pot on it"). Do not rewrite stack, open or close into other verbs.
12. Do not rewrite these interchangeable words and do not add locator detail the incoming instruction lacks: cabinet/cupboard (with or without "wooden"), mug/cup when the object is a mug, bottom/lower, top/upper, middle/centre, back/rear, compartment/slot, beside/next to, in front of/to the front of, to the right of/on the right of, "both"/"the" for a pair of objects. None of them changes the outcome; extra words such as "open", "on the left" or "white" for a cabinet neither help nor hurt, so keep the instruction's own wording there.
13. A landmark that locates the target object (the noun after "on", "next to", "in" or "beside" inside the object phrase, e.g. "the bowl next to the ramekin", "the bowl in the top drawer of the cabinet") may be changed only by rules 2 and 3 (scene name, "dish"); never drop, generalise or replace a landmark with a different object, because the landmark is what tells the robot which of several similar objects to pick.
14. Apply the rules in the order 1 to 13, make only the edits they require, and when the scene description does not settle which object or place an incoming word refers to, keep the incoming word unchanged; the robot tolerates most paraphrase, and a wrong referent costs the whole task.
